# NeuroTranslation

**What a hand has just done still beats a brain signal read at the scalp.**

**Start here:** 1. [Try {{ spell(try_rows('browser')) }} pages in your browser]({{ gh_anchor(TRY_HEADING()) }}) · 2. [Read every result](benchmarks/README.md) · 3. Reach for a cup. Your brain gives the order, the muscles of your forearm pull, and the hand moves: one act, written down three times, in nerve, in muscle and in motion. NeuroTranslation predicts one of those records from another, and checks each prediction against what the body actually did.

That began with {{ n_studies() }} [small studies](benchmarks/README.md). Each takes recordings that scientists made of people and shared openly, and predicts what a hand or an arm will do a fraction of a second later: a wrist's angle, a palm's position, a grip's force. Later studies follow a moving body from head to foot. A hand tends to keep moving the way it was moving, so a brain or muscle signal earns its place only by beating the hand's own recent past.

- Electrodes on the scalp pick up tiny voltages from the brain (EEG). Added to a prediction that already knew the hand's recent movement, EEG made it worse **{{ eeg_raised() }} times out of {{ C('eeg', 'n') }}**, and failed all {{ eeg_rules() }} of its pass rules, tests of success set before any scoring.
- Electrodes on the skin of the forearm pick up the electrical activity of its muscles (EMG). Added the same way, EMG lowered the error **{{ C('emg', 'lower') }} times out of {{ C('emg', 'n') }}**, but only at the wrist was the gain clearly larger than the differences between people.
- A computer model that learned only from the recent past of whatever it predicted beat repeating the last value in **{{ C('carry_home', 'fitted_lower') }} of {{ C('carry_home', 'n') }}** studies, on the task it learned: {{ S(1).cmp(0, 'fitted', 2) }} against {{ S(1).cmp(0, 'rule', 2) }} at the wrist.
- When the task changed, or the person had to follow a new force level shown on a screen, a one-line rule such as "repeat the last value" beat the best learned model in **{{ C('shifted_settings', 'best_fitted_beaten_by_a_one_line_rule') }} of {{ C('shifted_settings', 'n') }}** cases.{{ shifted_words_check() }}

The project is also building one general model, **NeuroTranslate 0.0**, meant to learn from many kinds of body signal. In [{{ unseen_count() }} tests on people nobody had looked at]({{ gh_anchor(GENERAL_HEADING) }}), simpler forecasts matched or beat it {{ unseen_lost() }} times; it won {{ unseen_won('pedal_load') }}, forecasting the load on a pedal from the pedal's own readings as people tried to lift the forefoot against it, in a test left incomplete because one of its eight recordings was unusable; the shin muscle's signal made it worse. So nothing here yet translates between nerve, muscle and movement.{{ general_worse_check() }}{{ back_checks() }}{{ first_fits() }}{{ force_checks() }}{{ arm_transfer_checks() }}

**Next Neuron Prediction** asks which recorded neuron will fire the next spike, given the activity so far. [What counts as the next neuron?](docs/next-neuron-prediction.md) · [Replay a spike history](https://eliminatenoise.github.io/NeuroTranslation/next-neuron-prediction/) · [See the test in another rat](https://eliminatenoise.github.io/NeuroTranslation/papers/next-neuron-prediction/).

How much earlier movement would a model need when it meets a new body? The landmark experiment tests camera-tracked movement. For neural signals, the aim is to test recordings across vertebrate clades and classes, with different individuals, tasks, sites and recording methods. Invertebrate recordings are optional. To test their value, train otherwise matched models with and without them, then compare their predictions on the same held-out vertebrates. [Try the model on moving points](https://eliminatenoise.github.io/NeuroTranslation/first-use-body/) · [Read the animal study](https://eliminatenoise.github.io/NeuroTranslation/papers/first-use-body/) · [How to test first use across body plans](docs/body-adaptation.md).

## History did the work: the numbered table

Each row sets a reference forecast against a challenger that was given something extra. Some rows forecast a movement, some the force at a gauge, some the voltage of a force sensor. The studies published after the table are on [the studies page](benchmarks/README.md), in no row and no count here.

Every score is an error: how far the forecasts fell from what was measured. Lower is better. Rows 1 to 13, 28 and 29 are scored by the measure that punishes large misses, rows 14 to 23, 26 and 27 by the plain average miss, and rows 24 and 25 by a squared error in units of their own.{{ measures_check() }} Row 20 is the odd one: it compares the width of bands, not errors. Units change from block to block, so compare along a row, never down a column.

A *straight-line fit* is the plainest kind of model, a weighted sum of its inputs. A *learned forecast* is a small network that reads a stretch of recent history. A *transformer* is a kind of network that learns which of the earlier readings matter most to the forecast, the same kind that runs today's chatbots: study 26 tried a small one, and the general model is built round one too. The recordings a model is scored on were never used to fit it, except in rows 25 and 27. Row 29 keeps each person out of the fit that scores them, but its six people are the ones studies 1 to 3 and 28 were fitted on, so it is not a test either.

## {{ GENERAL_HEADING }}

NeuroTranslate 0.0 is meant to be a *large movement model*: one model that learns how nerve, muscle and movement go together from many kinds of recording at once. Most studies above fitted a small model of their own, for one signal and one task. Each time the general model learns from a set of recordings is one fit.

It has had its first runs on walking, wrists, a rat, knees, the force under a walker's foot, an arm following a cue, a sensor on a person's back, a muscle in the shin seen by ultrasound, the load on a pedal, a walker's heel, a walker told how fast to walk and a walker's balance on a beam. {{ unseen_count(True) }} times it was scored on people nobody had looked at when the test was fixed, and simpler forecasts matched or beat it in {{ unseen_lost() }} of them:

- Given a leg's muscle signals, and scored on [eight walkers whose recordings nobody had inspected when the test was designed](models/neurotranslate-0.0/right-plate/), it did a little better with them, but a straight-line fit given the same signals did better still, and the gain failed the test set for it in advance.{{ U('right-plate') }}
- Fitted on seven people's arms and scored on [five others whose recordings were opened only after the test was fixed](models/neurotranslate-0.0/arm-transfer/), it beat the same model fitted on one person, but predicting each cue's usual change, or no change at all, did better still.{{ U('arm-transfer') }}
- It did only as well as a straight-line fit at forecasting how far a sensor on [three new people's backs](models/neurotranslate-0.0/back_rotation/) would turn, and worse once given voltages from the skin over the spine.{{ U('back_rotation') }}
- At forecasting how much a muscle in [four new people's shins](models/neurotranslate-0.0/muscle_tracking/), filmed by ultrasound, would lengthen or shorten, it lost to a straight-line fit.{{ U('muscle_tracking') }}
- At forecasting how far the head and the base of the spine of [six new people](models/neurotranslate-0.0/visual_balance/) would move in the next eighth of a second after the view in their headset rotated, as they walked a narrow beam, it lost to a straight-line fit given only their recent movement and the rotation's direction, and voltages from the scalp made it worse.{{ U('visual_balance') }}{{ balance_checks() }}

### A translation needs both texts from the same moment

The general model is meant to learn from four kinds of data.

| Kind of data | What it is | What it is not |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Neural recordings** | Electrical activity of the nervous system, at the places where the electrodes sat: brain, spinal cord or the nerves of the body. | A recording at one place is not a recording at another. EEG from the scalp says nothing direct about a nerve in the arm. |
| **Muscle recordings** | EMG: the electrical activity of muscle, picked up on the skin over it. | A recording of muscle, not of nerve. Not a force either: getting force out of EMG takes a second measurement or a mechanical model. |
| **Motion capture** | Where the body went: joint angles and hand positions, with their units and their clock. | The movement itself. It is a tracker's estimate, with the tracker's error. |
| **Physics-based simulation of the body** | Movement and force computed from a model of bones, joints and muscles. | Measured. A simulation is tested against a measurement of the same task, never the other way round. |

A fifth thing is needed, and it is not data. It is the pairing.

Suppose the motion recording belongs to a different reach. It can still teach a model what reaches look like. It cannot say where *this* hand was when *this* nerve fired. A translation can be checked only when both recordings come from the same person, the same movement and the same clock, and even two files that name the same clock can be offset from each other. Every study above uses signals that were recorded together.

### Learning two datasets at once has not yet shown a gain

The project's working presumption is that more data, and more varied data, makes a model that carries further. The general model's shared fit compared learning two datasets with learning one: it learned a headset's tracking of people's wrists and a camera's tracking of a rat together. The wrist-and-sacrum study in the table above later did the same with a small transformer of its own, which learned a lifting wrist and a balancing body, and its test failed.

## Three projects, one question

NeuroTranslation is one of three projects in one programme, Brain2Body, that ask the same question: what passes between the brain and the body? The brain sends orders out to the muscles, and the body reports back what it is doing and feeling. Each project comes at that traffic from a different side.

- **NeuroTranslation**, this project, predicts one recording of the body from another, most of them of a movement: brain, nerve, muscle, force or motion, recorded by scientists who share their data openly. Every prediction is checked against what was actually recorded, and so far a signal's own recent past, above all a hand's own recent movement, has been the forecast to beat. [Open its pages in your browser](https://eliminatenoise.github.io/NeuroTranslation/START.html)
- **[NeuroSurge](https://github.com/eliminatenoise/NeuroSurge)** is a game for learning which part of the brain moves which part of the body. You look out from inside a body without skin. Turn your view to a coloured area of the brain in front of you, and the body part it controls moves; click a muscle and its area lights up. What the game's brain does is made up by the game's rules, not read from anyone's brain. [Play NeuroSurge in your browser](https://eliminatenoise.github.io/NeuroSurge/)
- **[Interoceptomics](https://github.com/eliminatenoise/Interoceptomics)** is a proposed field of science that would measure what the body senses of its own interior, a heartbeat or a breath, and then ask which nerve connections carry it. None of its experiments has been run yet. Two of its pages run in your browser. [The experiment guide](https://eliminatenoise.github.io/Interoceptomics/stages/) walks through the proposed experiments one step at a time, each beside a 3D sketch you can turn. [The atlas viewer](https://eliminatenoise.github.io/Interoceptomics/atlas/) is a 3D model of the left half of a human brain that you can turn, cut and measure. It is built from the Allen Institute's published map of the brain's regions: a map, not a measurement.

They are joined by a plan and a guide.

- The game is meant to become the place where NeuroTranslation's model is used. The plan: type or say an action, or move in front of your camera, and the body on screen does it, driven by NeuroTranslate 0.0, the one model NeuroTranslation is building to learn from many kinds of body signal at once. None of this is in the published game. A first draft is [a proposed change to the game](https://github.com/eliminatenoise/NeuroSurge/pull/2), not yet merged: commands you type or speak choose from a set of the game's own movements, an arm or knee on screen follows yours as your camera sees it, and a map inside the game opens the recordings NeuroTranslation studies and its fitted models.
- The experiment guide lays out the proposed experiments step by step, in two halves. Human movement (the "Small vision") holds the movement tasks in people, where NeuroTranslation and the game meet; Animal learning (the "Big vision") is Interoceptomics' road map: six proposed stages of experiments in animals, in 22 numbered steps. Several of NeuroTranslation's examples already sit inside the guide, among them two forecasts a quarter of a second ahead: the two angles of a person's right wrist as they steer a cursor to a target, from three of its models, and where a rat on a W-shaped track will be.
