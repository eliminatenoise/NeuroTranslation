NeuroTranslation and Brain2Body: the four papers

Two papers, each written twice: a Technical edition, and a Simple English edition that explains the same things in ordinary words. Start with a Simple English edition.

NeuroTranslation: A New Paradigm In Brain-Body Mapping.

NeuroTranslation tests how much brain and muscle recordings add to forecasts that already use recent movement. It reports all {{ n_in_papers() }} studies in the numbered table. Chapter 7 adds walking forecasts and a separate Next Neuron Prediction test on one rat recording.

Brain2Body: The Next Generation In Body-Brain Interfacing.

Brain2Body proposes testing what a person retains after a machine stops guiding their hand through a skill. The paper reports no experiment on skill retention.

Animal tracking and first-use forecasts. Watch the model follow the points already visible. Change the shape of the figure, hide its future and compare the forecast with where the points actually land.

Next Neuron Prediction. The original study scores forecasts from two rats. Try a fictional recording or compare the turtle and salamander tests.

A wrist forecast on a different person. A camera followed {{ N(CROSS_WRIST, 'selected') }} annotated left-wrist reaches from the only person held out. Continuing a fraction of the wrist’s earlier speed beat the regression at both forecast times.

Two wrists on a second recording day. One person makes ordinary reaching movements on two days. This comparison tests earlier video and electrical measurements against forecasts from movement alone.

The reaching model, one week later. The study tested adding earlier arm movement to forecasts of brain activity when the same animal returned. The result differed between workspaces. A second comparison follows changes in 39 modeled muscle–tendon paths.

Reaching and sensory cortex. One recording lets us compare the measured path of the arm, its forecast, and the spikes that followed.

Forecasting a cylinder’s turn. Later repetitions in the same volunteers test whether a fitted model can make use of acceleration in addition to earlier turning rates.

Reach forecasts with and without the target. Human transport and prompted gestures, with the cue and measured endpoint defined separately for each experiment.

The same electrodes, one day later. A held-day test of whether handle movement adds to earlier electrical counts and cues.

When the arm is moved. A matched comparison of neural, cue and handle histories in 105 later trials from one macaque.

From stretch to spikes. The spike-history forecast had lower count deviance than a model that also read tendon mechanics, in an exploratory comparison of three sensory fibers.

Movement forecasts with and without scalp recordings. Compare the correctly timed recording with an earlier trial from the same person.

Shared and separate models for wrist and pelvis movement. Two equally sized models turn earlier movement and muscle recordings into 125 milliseconds of forecast motion.

A shared model for wrist and spine-base movement. The wrist result depended on the training budget. The balance specialist followed the sacrum more closely. Download the paper.

A shared model for leg-muscle activity and pelvis movement. Two specialist models had the lower average errors on 1,759 standing and walking rotations. Download the paper.

Walking balance forecasts with and without scalp signals. In 880 recorded rotations, neither marker met the test for a benefit from scalp input. Download the paper.

Walking forecasts with a speed instruction. Ten people, two cues and 60 walks put a task-trained Transformer and the shared movement encoder to the test. Download the paper.

Heel movement and upward force forecasts. Two forecasts from the same step put a joint Transformer and the general movement encoder to separate tests. Download the illustrated PDF.

Pedal load forecasts with a shin muscle signal. Four volunteers pulled against a fixed pedal. An ambiguous recording left the planned two-person test incomplete. Download the illustrated PDF.

Shin muscle-fibre length forecasts. Two models forecast a change in length from ultrasound tracking. The study compares forecasts with and without the angle from the same recording. Download the illustrated PDF.

Back-sensor rotation a tenth of a second ahead. Two forecasts test whether voltages recorded on the back add to what recent motion already tells us. Download the illustrated PDF.

Neck-electrode voltage four milliseconds ahead. Two models forecast a small change in surface voltage. Matching the upper-arm signal to the right stimulus tests what that electrode contributes. Download the illustrated PDF.

Arm forecasts with and without early scalp readings. Two families of models predict the later elbow, forearm and finger readings. Eye electrodes and recordings from other repetitions of the same task help test what the scalp contributes. Download the illustrated PDF.

Forecasting cued arm movements in new people. Seven people supplied the fitting recordings. Five others supplied the recorded test of the cue, early motion and a first-run correction. Download the PDF.

Cued arm forecasts. Two experiments compare what an instruction, recent movement and a scalp electrode contribute to a right-arm forecast. Download the cued-arm study as a PDF.

Wrist position a fifth of a second ahead. The study compares two forecasts of skin-marker movement during slow walking. Download the walking-marker study as a PDF.

One stride, two force plates reports two later experiments on overground walking. Read the supplement as a PDF.
