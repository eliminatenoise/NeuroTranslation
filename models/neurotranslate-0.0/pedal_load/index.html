What will the pedal read next?

A volunteer tries to lift their forefoot against a fixed pedal. Electrodes on the shin pick up a voltage from the muscle. A second sensor records the load on the pedal. Can the earlier muscle signal help predict a change in that reading?

These models see 201 consecutive samples of past measurements and forecast the load-channel change 100 sample positions later. The recording system sampled at a reported 1,000 times per second. Some files carry a time vector; for others, the nominal time is calculated from row order. Individual hardware intervals have not been verified.

The output uses the original load sensor’s scale. Converting it to force would require the measured calibration of the signal conditioner. The study’s 0.1 m platform lever could then convert calibrated force into a net ankle moment. It does not tell us the force generated by one muscle.

How the response model works

Take the absolute size of the muscle voltage at each sample. One running average responds quickly, another slowly. The response model learns a target load level from these two quantities and the pedal setting. It predicts part of the change toward that level over the next 100 samples. The chosen time constants describe a model assumption. They are not measurements of a volunteer’s muscle.

What did the usable trials show?

Errors are mean absolute differences on the load channel’s original scale. Smaller is closer. Each person, setting and trial has equal weight at its own level. The two held participants supplied separate Task 1 recordings; their participant codes also occur in an earlier muscle-imaging study.
