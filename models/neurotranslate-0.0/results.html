Where will the wrist be in a quarter of a second?

Six people steered a cursor by turning their right wrist. Two encoders measured its bend and sideways tilt. Forearm electrodes helped a model trained on movement forecast those angles. Adding scalp electrodes gave back most of the gain.

Smaller errors mean closer forecasts. Each person took a turn as the test participant, with the other five supplying the fit. Each of the six counts equally. The angular inputs and outputs received the same training in both columns. The temporal weights stayed fixed.

Does the recorded signal change the answer?

Give the forearm model a different trial’s voltages at the same time after the cue. The forecast changes by 2.723° root mean square, and its error against the encoders rises by 0.815°. Swapping the scalp voltages in the full model moves the forecast by 1.278° and raises error by 0.096°. Co-occurring movement and changes in electrode contact can contribute to these effects.

Both sets of electrodes together lowered error by 0.012° against the motion-only fit. Only three of the six people improved. An earlier wrist-specific network scored 3.618° from motion alone and 3.549° with a forearm correction. The reused distance weights offered no advantage over the single random control in these comparisons.

Can the forearm correct the wrist forecast?

First make the shared model forecast from motion alone. Correct that forecast using the wrist history and its error falls from 4.503° to 4.175°. Give the same correction the forearm signals and it falls to 4.054°. Four of the six people improved over the motion correction. The earlier wrist-specific network with its forearm correction still led at 3.549°.

Swap the forearm recording for another trial and error rises to 4.419°. Reverse the sixteen forearm bins and it rises to 4.305°. Replace the bins by their mean and it rises to 4.170°. The clocks mattered on these six people. Run python predict.py forearm/example.json to try the shared correction with invented inputs. One model file, three measured movements

The original settings forecast straight-line displacement in centimetres for an optically tracked human wrist or a camera-tracked rat. The new setting reads a separate pointing experiment and returns two angles in degrees. All three use the same temporal attention weights. The original outputs matched exactly on 583 human and 106 rat test windows.

The pointing task supplied 1,879 usable trials and six overlapping forecast windows per trial. We used only these six previously studied people. The separate exploratory and reserved groups stayed outside this comparison. The forecast spans 128 stored ticks at 512 Hz. Physical sensor latency remains unknown.

Does a rat’s firing sharpen its distance forecast?

For three rats outside adapter training, camera history alone gave an average error of 2.805 cm. Giving the shared model hippocampal and prefrontal firing lowered it to 2.778 cm. Firing from an earlier trip scored 2.804 cm. KL8 alone improved over both controls. The straight-line speed forecast still did better for that rat. One scrambled encoder with the same fitting procedure averaged 2.763 cm. The earlier benchmark had already reported the three rats’ results.

Run python predict.py population/example.json in this folder to forecast from JS21’s recorded movement and firing. The model gives 6.998 cm against 1.831 cm measured by the camera. Explore the observed window, controls and individual animals.
