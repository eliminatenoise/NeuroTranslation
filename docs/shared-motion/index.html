Input units, channels and clock

Use wrist_angle for the domain. The cutoff angles in anchorDeg are extension and radial deviation, in degrees. In every bin, the first two columns are the mean angles minus those cutoff angles. Use mode motion for these two columns. Mode emg adds four root-mean-square amplitudes in µV, each from the left-minus-right difference of forearm pair M1, M2, M3 or M4, in order. Mode full adds eight signed mean scalp voltages in µV, in the order C3, C1, Cz, C2, C4, CP3, CPz, CP4.

Each bin spans 16 stored samples at 512 Hz. Its final sample belongs to the bin. Enter the 16 ordered integer end ticks in binEndTicks. The last equals cutoffTick. For slot 0 through 5, the cutoff is cueTick + 256 + 128 × slot. Only include earlier measurements through that cutoff. Mode and column count must agree. The browser can check the declared timing. You must confirm how the samples were acquired and binned.

The viewer runs every forecast your channels support. A saved input can also specify forearmResidual. For an adjustment based on movement, set both that field and mode to motion. For an adjustment that also uses the forearm electrodes, set both to emg. Leave the field out for a direct forecast.

What does knowing the instruction add?

A participant saw one of seven cues while their right arm rested in a support. The study recorded the elbow and forearm with the support and the base of the index finger with a glove. This reader forecasts each instrument’s change half a second, one second and one and a half seconds after its last input.

Across 126 trials in three runs, the shared encoder did better with the correct cue than with the cue removed or replaced. The cue alone gave the lowest average error. These are held-run comparisons within one person. The words in the box select a known task. The original record identifies the visual cue’s category.

The input ends a quarter second after the stored cue. Each of four successive 125 ms bins contains three sensor medians, followed by the three differences between the second-half and first-half medians. This branch uses the mechanical sensors. A separate study examines scalp and eye recordings. Try the scalp-and-elbow study · Study, source and limitations.

Can one rule follow another body location?

Nine positions describe the last 400 milliseconds of one skin marker. The model learned from markers on the outer ankles, outer knees and thumb side of the wrists. We tested the same rule on 46 other locations in 10 walkers.

Across those 46 locations, the shared encoder missed the future point by 31.00 mm on average. Continuing the last velocity missed by 43.29 mm. A separate fit that compared the movement with training examples came closest at 28.73 mm. The public reader offers the six methods that can run without distributing individual training examples. The study code reproduces all seven from the original data.

The cameras tracked skin markers. The released positions were reconstructed and smoothed using samples on both sides of each time point. The result describes the stored position 20 camera frames (200 milliseconds) later. Methods and results by body location.

What reaches the plate after heel strike?

Two markers track the right heel and forefoot through four short intervals. Eight electrodes on the same leg record muscle activity. The model can use the markers alone or add the muscle signals to estimate the vertical load ten frames later. The reference is the mean of 15 force samples from one platform whose location and right-foot contact were checked at heel strike. Other loads elsewhere on the walkway fall outside this measurement.

Of ten test walkers, eight provided 53 eligible windows from 16 walks. With muscle signals added, the shared model missed by 85.18 N on average. Using markers with the same encoder missed by 93.93 N. Five of eight walkers improved, but the paired 95% interval for the difference spanned −28.99 to +9.03 N. A linear fit with both signals scored 82.04 N. The tests did not establish an advantage for the shared encoder or a consistent contribution from the correctly timed muscle signal.

The source was processed across time in both directions and the muscle recordings were centred across the trial. These forecasts use the stored clock. Live performance and biological lead time remain unknown.

Can angles tell us how hard the leg turns?

Optical markers track the motion. Force plates record the load on the ground. Together these measurements let scientists estimate the net turning effect around each knee and divide it by the person’s mass. The model gets only the angles. It estimates the turning moment a quarter second later in newton-metres per kilogram (N·m/kg).

Seven people supplied the training walks. Across 1,207 forecasts for three other walkers at a nominal 0.8 m/s, the task-trained model missed by 0.08874 N·m/kg on average. A linear fit scored 0.09761 and matching the motion to training walkers’ steps scored 0.09373. The task-trained model beat both for just one walker. These are offline comparisons with inverse-dynamics estimates. The sign and size of the moment cannot identify the force from an individual muscle. Explore the knee moment comparison.

Does learning from wrists and rats help with knees?

People walked on a treadmill at 0.5 m/s. Sensors on their bodies supplied reconstructed joint angles. The model sees 240 consecutive frames from the two knees and looks for an earlier stretch of the walk that resembles the last second. Repeating that stride gives the first forecast. Three fitted models each try to correct it using information available by the last frame.

The shared model keeps the weights learned from wrist and rat movement. It missed by 3.465° on average across the two knees of 12 previously studied people. Fitting the earlier angles alone scored 3.460°, shuffling the shared weights before fitting scored 3.422°, and repeating the stride scored 3.535°. Each person counts equally across 40,201 predictions in total. The trained weights showed no advantage over both fitted alternatives. How accurately the model forecasts live movement or a new cohort remains unknown.

Did the electrodes help?

Six people steered a cursor with their right wrist. Two encoders on the pointing device measured the angles. Forearm electrodes recorded muscle electrical activity (EMG), and scalp electrodes recorded voltage (EEG). The forecast uses only the preceding half-second and the timing of the cue.

On the same windows from six previously studied people, the shared Transformer’s motion-only correction scored 4.175°. Giving the correction the four forearm signals brought that down to 4.054°. Four of the six people improved. In the three direct forecasts, adding forearm signals helped five people. Adding scalp voltages to those signals raised error for all six.

A separate wrist-specific convolutional network (CNN) learns short patterns in wrist movement. With its own forearm correction it scored 3.549° on the same windows. You can run that model in Python. Its accuracy on a new person is unknown, as is the accuracy of the shared model’s final fit. Download the wrist-specific Python model (ZIP) · Forearm comparison and scores.

What the three forecasts share

The wrist and rat distance tasks taught a Transformer to read four successive pieces of movement. We kept 5,016 learned values in that temporal core fixed. Four new wrist-angle bins make one piece, so the same core can read sixteen bins. Each set of channels has its own fitted input projection and output for the two angles. A change between rows therefore reflects the different fitted models as well as their inputs.

The forearm recording measures electrical activity at the skin. The scalp recording measures voltage at electrodes. Movement or changes in electrode contact can enter either. A predicted wrist angle tells us how the model expects the wrist to turn. The experiment did not measure intent, spinal signals, nerve traffic, force or a causal path from brain to muscle.

How far do the results travel?

Each test held out one whole person and fitted the new weights using the other five. The six had all contributed to earlier development. There were 1,879 eligible trials and six overlapping forecast windows per trial, giving 11,274 windows. Selecting complete trials required seeing that their eventual measurements existed. Live coverage and hardware delay remain unknown. The displayed fits used all six people and await an independent test.

With both electrical recordings, average error fell just 0.012° compared with movement alone. Three people improved. Omitting one person at a time gave differences from −0.087° to +0.069°. One matched randomly initialized temporal core had lower error in all three comparisons. This study found no advantage from learning the earlier distance tasks.

Did learning together help?

The same learned weights read both histories. Only the final calculation changes between a wrist tracked in three dimensions and a rat tracked in two. Both contribute movement during training. Does adding the other domain improve the prediction?

Neither domain passed the transfer test. On six wrist recording groups the shared model beat the simple movement rules and both linear models on average. On nine later trips from the same rat, keeping the latest speed already gave a smaller average error.

What counted as transfer?

We compared the shared model with two Transformers trained on just one domain. At each update one saw the same 32 examples from that domain. The other saw 64, matching the shared model’s total examples per update. Transfer required a lower error than both, with both upper limits of the paired 95% intervals below zero. Both comparisons also had to favor joint training at each of two further random starts.

The 583 wrist histories came from six acquisition groups, which may include the same people. The 106 rat histories came from nine trips by one animal. These reused assessment groups were separate from training. Resampling groups for the wrist and blocks of three successive trips for the rat describes variation within these recordings. Transfer to new people, animals or tasks remains untested.

How the model reads movement

Five positions become four intervals. For each interval the model uses the distance between its endpoints, elapsed recording time, the age of its final sample, distance across the last two intervals and how sharply the movement turned. The model also derives a speed scaled to a quarter second. The first interval carries zeros for the two features that require an earlier step.

A temporal Transformer lets each of the four past intervals draw on all four. The shared layer combines them into one learned representation. A wrist or rat readout then adjusts the latest-speed estimate. The future measured position enters only when scoring a prediction. Training used a nominal lead of 0.25 s. The recording clocks put the admitted endpoints 0.20–0.30 s after the last observed position.

The recording supplies positions alone. Neural activity, muscle force and intended movements are outside this experiment.
