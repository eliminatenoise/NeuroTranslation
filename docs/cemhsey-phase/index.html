Learning the squeeze and the release

On the screen, the target rose and fell at the same speed. We used six pinches from three adults to learn how force followed that cue. Then three different adults supplied six test pinches. The forecasts used the learned corrections without fitting to those six test recordings.

What does a second coefficient buy?

Each set of three bars starts from the same forecast. Read across to see how its error changes with one correction or with separate corrections for rising and falling force cues. Shorter bars mean smaller average error.

Who gained from the change?

A smaller average error can coexist with a worse forecast for one or two people. Compare two forecasts made on the same test readings. A positive gain means the selected forecast had a lower mean absolute error.

What the gauge measured

The gauge measured force between the pads of two digits. The target was 50% of each person’s maximum for that grasp on the same day. The simple forecast carries the latest gauge reading forward and adds the scheduled change over the next 24 force samples. The history model uses recent force and cue phase. The five-grid model also uses forearm surface EMG.

Each learned correction multiplies the coming change in the cue. The split model uses a different multiplier according to the direction of that change. With a flat cue, both corrections leave the parent forecast untouched.

Errors are calculated for each person using both test repeats together, then averaged across the three people. Signed error is forecast minus gauge. The 24-sample lead is 120 milliseconds on the nominal recording clock. The study spans one target, one indexed day and one grasp.
