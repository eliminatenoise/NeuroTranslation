Hand position, 100 milliseconds ahead

On three days of recorded reaching, taking the midpoint between two forecasts reduced the average miss. One forecast used the hand’s recent speed and direction. The other used recorded brain activity. The combined forecast missed the hand’s ending point by 4.13, 5.75 and 3.16 mm on the three days.

These are recordings from one macaque. All three days had already been evaluated in earlier studies. The comparison uses saved forecasts and archived positions.

How the forecasts work

  • Stay put. The predicted ending point is the last measured position. If the hand actually moves 4 mm, this forecast misses by 4 mm.
  • Constant velocity. Find the change between 40 ms ago and forecast time. Multiply that change by 2.5 to extend the same speed and direction to 100 ms ahead.
  • Constant acceleration. Use all three positions, including the one 80 ms ago. If the first and second 40 ms moves differ, carry their change in speed and direction forward. This fits a curve through the three past points.
  • Original neural forecast. Three versions of NeuroTranslate read 200 ms of recorded activity from electrodes in the part of the brain involved in movement. Each predicts a change in X and Y and gives a separate prediction of distance moved.
  • Published neural blend. The earlier method mixes each neural forecast with staying put. It sets the weights from past brain activity and agreement among its three models. The same three trained models and weights are used here.
  • Equal blend. Take the midpoint of the landing point from constant velocity and the one from the published neural blend. Do this separately for each of the three neural forecasts. The midpoint is the new landing point.

The earlier base models use 96 electrodes and ten groups of readings, each covering 20 ms. They were fitted on 23 September 2010. The earlier blend weights were set using positions from 20 and 22 September. The equal blend uses one half from each forecast on every row, with fixed weights. Earlier model and weights

What the error measures

Ending position gives the straight-line distance from the forecasted point to the measured point. Distance moved gives the difference between the forecasted and measured lengths of the movement. For example, forecast 10 mm left when the hand goes 10 mm right and the distance error is zero. The ending-point error is 20 mm.

We used 130,723 forecasts from 9,075 trials, taken on 12 August, 19 August and 10 September 2010. Each trial contributes equally. For the neural methods, we average the errors from the three models. The 95% range in the comparison comes from resampling whole trials 4,000 times within each day. It describes the variation within that day.

After forecasting, we also grouped the scores by how far the hand actually moved. The group marked “afterwards” uses the later position. The group marked “before” uses the difference over the previous 40 ms. Both are ways to examine the same forecasts.

The equal blend reduced the ending-position miss by 1.07, 0.30 and 1.74 mm versus constant velocity. On 19 August it also raised the distance-moved error by 0.08 mm. On 10 September the published neural blend had the smallest distance error. Timing is based on the archived samples. A live sensor setup or a new animal would need its own check.
