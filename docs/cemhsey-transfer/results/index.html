At a new pinch strength, a one-line rule beat the fitted model for all four people

A model trained at 30% of maximum voluntary contraction faced three targets. We asked whether the scheduled cue helped, and whether one practice pinch helped again. Four people each performed both repeats at every target on the same indexed recording day.

At 10% and 50% of the hardest pinch, the rule “current force plus the change in the on-screen cue” missed by less than every fitted forecast: 0.37 against at best 0.56 percentage points of the hardest pinch at 10%, and 0.57 against at best 0.70 at 50%. It beat each of the four cue-aware fitted forecasts for all four people. At 30%, the target the model was trained on, the fitted forecasts did better (0.43 against 0.45).

Which forecast missed by less?

The first repeat supplied the practice measurements. This comparison uses only the second repeat. Each dot gives the average change in mean absolute error across the four people. The line joins the smallest and largest of their four changes. A positive value favors the candidate.

MAE, RMSE and signed error are in MVC fraction. Positive signed error means the forecast was above the gauge. The last column divides MAE by the prescribed peak, so errors at a light pinch can also be read relative to that smaller target.

What does practice change?

The scheduled cue rises, holds, then falls. Its upcoming change is known before the later force reading. The target adjustment changes the 30% forecast according to what the cue will do over the next 24 force samples. The practice version learns one additional multiplier from that person’s first repeat at that target.

The nominal forecast horizon is 120 milliseconds on the released force clock. These are overlapping cutoffs from four people on one day. The source cannot separate a target effect from all effects of trial order, and the four observed differences give no confidence interval for a wider population.
