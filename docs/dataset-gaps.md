# What did the hand feel when the object began to slip?

A grip sensor measures how hard the fingers squeeze. The skin can already be sliding at the rim of the contact while the centre still sticks. **The first dataset I would add pairs images of that slip with impulses recorded from a sensory nerve fibre in the same human fingertip.** It gives NeuroTranslation a new question with an independently observed answer. Our grip-and-lift recordings already have force, finger motion, scalp signals and muscle voltage. The rat spindle recordings cover a different source of feedback, a receptor in muscle. [Grip after contact](https://eliminatenoise.github.io/NeuroTranslation/contact-force/) · [Rat spindle data](https://doi.org/10.5061/dryad.vdncjsxsw) · [Human fingertip experiment](https://elifesciences.org/articles/64679)

| Priority | Measurements worth pairing | A test someone else could score |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **1. Skin slip and sensory spikes** | Delhaye and colleagues imaged the fingertip against a moving transparent surface while recording single tactile fibres and contact forces. The images show where the skin strains under load. | Predict the fibre's next spike count from earlier force and firing. Add the earlier local skin strain and compare count error on withheld trials across friction conditions. Keep each fibre's trials together when testing transfer to a new fibre. [Zenodo deposit, CC BY 4.0](https://zenodo.org/records/4818439) |
| **2. Motor-unit firing and graded force** | Avrillon and colleagues recorded people pushing with the ankle or knee at 10–80% of their maximum. The deposit includes measured force, the requested force trace and firing times estimated for individual motor units from high-density muscle electrodes. It separates what the person was asked to produce from what the instrument registered. | Hold out a contraction intensity. Compare forecasts of the achieved force from past force and the requested level, then add muscle voltage or motor-unit firing separately. Score each person equally in percent of maximum after verifying the scale. The output is the force at the apparatus. [Original data, CC BY 4.0](https://doi.org/10.6084/m9.figshare.24640944.v1) · [Study](https://doi.org/10.7554/eLife.97085.3) |
| **3. Reported aim, cursor error and actual reach** | A target tells us where a task asks someone to go. When the displayed cursor is rotated, the person can deliberately aim somewhere else. Wilterson and Taylor recorded the location tapped before the reach and the subsequent hand trajectory. | On a later day, predict initial hand heading in degrees from the target and previous cursor error. Measure how much the person's pre-reach report changes that error. The report records a choice the person communicated. The public data folder lists rotation and mirror files, with reuse terms unconfirmed. [Methods](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8362683/) · [OSF deposit](https://osf.io/vtqa3/) |
| **4. Gaze, terrain and the next footfall** | Two people can walk toward the same destination and step on different stones. Muller and colleagues placed gaze, footfalls and a reconstructed walking surface in one coordinate system. The view ahead gives a stride model something concrete to choose among. | At the preceding footfall, predict the next landing position. Compare body motion and terrain alone with the same inputs plus earlier gaze. Report distance error on whole withheld walks. Use the terrain map as a surveyed surface and keep the walk's later gaze and body positions out of the inputs. [Dryad deposit, CC0](https://doi.org/10.5061/dryad.r7sqv9sn2) |
| **5. Repeated movements after an electrode is moved** | A different muscle voltage on a second visit could reflect movement, physiology or sensor placement. Rolandino's RPC-Net data include four people who repeated hand poses with the forearm array repositioned between trials. Recorded hand kinematics give a separate reference. | Fit on one placement, then estimate hand angles in degrees on another. Compare with held repetitions at the original placement where available. Keep whole placements separate and report each person's error. This measures sensitivity to repositioning under this protocol. [RPC-Net DS3.a, CC BY 4.0](https://zenodo.org/records/16789929) |
| **6. Measured load inside a moving joint** | A floor plate measures the force at the floor. An instrumented knee implant measures the load transmitted through that joint. CAMS-Knee paired implant forces and moments with dynamic X-ray tracking, floor forces, muscle voltages and body movement in six knee-replacement recipients. | Predict peak implant contact force in newtons on a withheld activity. Compare a model using floor forces and motion with one that also uses participant-specific implant geometry and tracked joint position. An example walk is public. The full comparison needs the registered institution's scientific-use agreement. [ETH Zurich data description](https://movement.ethz.ch/data-repository/cams-knee-project.html) · [Full-data terms](https://cams-knee.orthoload.com/wp-content/uploads/sites/4/Description_CAMS_Knee_Full_Dataset_v1.1.pdf) |

**One shortlisted source already deserves a closer look.** Kim and colleagues recorded shin ultrasound, muscle voltage, motion capture and floor forces during the same walks. Once the exported clocks are verified, one test could ask whether earlier ultrasound improves a held-person forecast of floor force at a different walking speed. It would extend our ankle-imaging work into loaded walking. The floor plate still leaves open how much tension any one muscle carried. [Walking data, CC BY 4.0](https://doi.org/10.6084/m9.figshare.28937612.v1) · [Acquisition methods](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41597-026-08156-5)

Each row proposes a separate experiment. None of these comparisons has been run here. The [source inventory](dataset-gaps-sources.json) identifies the records, access conditions and the measurements each one can support.
