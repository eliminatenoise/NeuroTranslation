Copying the last stride beat every angle-only forecast of the knee’s turning effect

A knee can be straightening while the muscles and tissues around it pull toward bending. How much can four seconds of leg angles tell us about that turning effect a quarter of a second later?

Each value is a net internal turning moment in newton-metres per kilogram of body mass. Its sign gives the direction of the turning effect. The angle trace gives the position of the joint.

What happened on real walks?

Seven people supplied the training walks. Three others supplied the test moments, across 1,207 overlapping windows of level treadmill walking at a nominal 0.8 m/s. An earlier study examined these same people's knee angles.

The task-trained Transformer had the lowest average error from angles alone. It beat the linear model for two of the three people and beat matching to a training walker for one. The frozen movement encoder beat both of its comparisons, the linear model and the same encoder with shuffled weights, for only one of the three people. Each model had to beat both of its comparisons for all three people; neither did.

What if you already know the moment from an earlier stride? On the 1,187 windows with the required earlier measurements, replaying the earlier moment gave 0.0678 N·m/kg error. The trained model gave 0.0888 on those same windows.

Smaller is closer. Average the absolute error across both knees within each person, then give each person equal weight.

The reference moments come from a model combining camera measurements with forces at the treadmill belts. They describe the combined turning effect across each knee. Individual muscle and joint contact forces remain unknown. The forecast targets later stored frames, and the live processing delay is unknown. Acquisition study · Original recordings

Emma Reznick, Kyle R. Embry, Ross Neuman, Edgar Bolívar, Nicholas P. Fey and Robert D. Gregg released the continuous treadmill recordings under CC0 1.0. The walking study fitted knee moment forecasts from prior optical angles, using inverse-dynamics moments as references. The browser includes fitted weights, aggregate results and an invented walk.
