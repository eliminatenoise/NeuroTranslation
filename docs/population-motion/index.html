How far will this rat move?

An overhead camera followed a rat on a W-shaped track. Pause the recording and use its last second of movement to forecast the next quarter-second. Does adding the firing of neurons in two brain regions bring the forecast closer?

Did firing help across rats?

The forecast had never seen any of these three rats during training. Correctly timed firing beat both movement alone and earlier-trip firing for one rat, KL8. Even there, continuing at the last measured speed gave the smallest error.

Each rat contributed 15 scored trips. Every trip counts equally within its rat, and each rat counts equally in the last row. The small average gain from adding firing (0.028 cm) hides the different outcomes in individual animals.

Shared movement encoder

The camera frames go through the same Transformer used for wrist and rat movement. Its attention sees only movement. After attention, the model pairs each region’s firing rate with the movement signal for that same quarter-second. The final readout predicts distance.

JS21 did better on movement alone. For JS34, firing from the earlier trip did a little better than firing from the right trip. Only KL8 improved on both checks, and continuing at its last speed still did better. Across the three rats, one encoder with its learned weights scrambled and its readout refitted scored 2.763 cm against the trained encoder’s 2.778 cm. The earlier direct-model results were already known when we designed this comparison.

Where the measurements came from

Position and time

The study tracked lights on the rat’s headstage with an overhead camera. Each point here is one archived XY position, in centimetres. Doubling back can leave a rat close to where it began, however far it ran along the way. The models predict only that separation.

At five quarter-second ticks, the model takes the latest camera sample available. To score a forecast we compare the final input position with the first frame at or after 250 ms beyond the cutoff. Frame timing varies. Across the three rats, the two measured positions were 250.4–320.7 ms apart.

What travels between rats?

The two areas are dorsal CA1, part of the hippocampus, and the prefrontal cortex (PFC). A recorded unit is a spike train separated from an electrode recording. Individual units belong to their own recording. What carries across rats is the rate per recorded unit in each area.

We fitted two ridge regressions using ER1, JS15 and JS17. One uses the movement history. The other adds four pairs of firing rates, each transformed as log(1 + rate). Replacing those eight values with an earlier trip tests how much the timing matters. These offline comparisons cannot establish that firing caused the movement.
