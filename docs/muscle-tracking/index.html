Can a muscle bundle’s angle help predict its length?

Lift your forefoot and the muscle on the front of your shin changes shape. Ultrasound can track a bundle of its fibres, called a fascicle. Given what the tracker has seen, how much longer or shorter will that bundle be ten tracking steps later?

How close were the forecasts?

Volunteers sat and raised the forefoot as a probe imaged the tibialis anterior, the muscle on the front of the shin. Here we can compare each forecast with the change the tracker actually recorded.

Four people held out from training

Both trials from each of these four people stayed out of fitting. We used the fitted model once on their eligible windows. Across the eight trials, 776 of 776 candidate windows contained the required consecutive image tracks. Several windows come from each person.

0.233 mm average error with earlier angle and length, 0.236 mm with length alone, and 0.270 mm with angles from the other trial.

The linear length model scored 0.223 mm. Repeating the last ten-step change scored 0.242 mm.

The model with matching angles beat the length-only Transformer for 3 of four people. It beat the same fit given angles from the other trial for 4 of four.

Adding earlier angle fell short of the planned test for an improvement in prediction.

The model with matching angles beat the linear length forecast in 3 of four people, but its average error across people was higher. Both tests were required.

What if we reuse a movement model?

A second forecast starts with an encoder trained on movement and fits a new readout. It groups the same history into four parts and uses angle through the cutoff. The two approaches therefore see different inputs.

Across the same four people, its mean error was 0.226 mm with length, 0.219 mm with length and angle, and 0.241 mm after swapping angles between trials. A linear fit using length and angle scored 0.215 mm. An untrained encoder with a fitted readout scored 0.222 mm.

Adding angle changed the average error by 2.9% relative to length alone (lower). The shared fit fell short of its test, which required at least 5% lower average error than both length alone and swapped angles, with a gain against both in at least three people.

What goes into one forecast?

Twenty steps of history

Take 21 lengths ending at the last available length. Take the 20 earlier angles at which the bundle meets a sheet of connective tissue. This is its pennation angle. Divide the history into five groups of four steps. Each supplies an ending length, a change in length, an average angle and a change in angle.

The Transformer learns how to weight those five groups. One fit uses length and angle. A second fit learns from length alone. Both predict a single signed change in millimetres. The graph adds that change to the last length to show the predicted endpoint.

Does the angle have to match?

In the recording test we keep the lengths in place and borrow angles from the same person’s other trial, at the closest proportion of the way through the eligible cutoffs. We then run the original length-and-angle fit again. The two trials share no image frames. In the example above, both angle histories are invented.

The straight-line comparison makes a different assumption. It projects the bundle onto a fixed reference axis using its length and angle, repeats the recent change in that projection and converts it back to millimetres along the bundle. It becomes undefined when the last angle’s cosine has magnitude below 0.1.

What did the probe measure?

Both length and angle come from the same ultrasound images and can share tracking errors. Some files give the reported pennation angle without a separate measurement of the reference sheet’s orientation. One example contains 992 original tracking rows and a 9,910-value curve interpolated from them. Counting that longer curve as new measurements would make the tracker look far more precise than it was. Matching these tracking steps to the muscle voltage and ankle-angle clocks would require a verified frame-by-frame timing map. In development, two trials contain 9,920 original tracking rows each and the other six contain 992 each. Ten tracking steps can cover different stretches of a recording. Their elapsed time in seconds remains unverified.

These recordings support an offline comparison of image-derived geometry. Muscle force, the direction of neural drive and a patient’s ability to control a device fall outside what it measures.
