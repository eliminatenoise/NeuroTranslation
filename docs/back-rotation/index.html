What happened on recorded movement?

Volunteers wore two grids of 32 electrodes, one on the upper back and one on the lower back. They also wore an orientation sensor. Its reading at the forecast cutoff and its reading 100 milliseconds later give us the turn to check against.

The planned four-person test is incomplete

In both trials from one of the four new people, the file labels three channels as orientation components 2, 3 and 4. It labels the remaining channel “BufferChannel”. Calculating a turn requires all four orientation components. The identity of the missing component could not be verified, so those two trials supply no verified turn to score.

Both model studies required all four people. Neither has a formal pass or fail result on its planned assessment.

Three new people with verified orientations

These six trials describe the three people whose readings could be checked. All models kept their original weights. For each forecast, the error is the distance in degrees between prediction and measurement. We average the moving and standing errors equally for each person, then average the three people.

The specialist model scored 0.361° from motion alone, 0.382° with both grids and 0.381° with the electrodes from another time. A simple forecast that repeats the turn observed over the preceding 100 ms scored 0.445°.

What if the model starts with an existing movement encoder?

A second model reused weights from an existing movement Transformer and fitted a new readout. Its four time slices and its voltage summaries differ from the specialist model above. For its time-swap comparison, the electrodes came from the same person doing the same task in the other trial.

Across the same three people, its balanced error was 0.376° from motion, 0.385° with both grids, and 0.385° with swapped grids.

A version with one untrained set of encoder weights scored 0.382°. The full linear model scored 0.380°. Both comparisons used the same input features as the pretrained model.

Development results before the final test

Four people in development

For each turn at testing, one development volunteer was left out and the model learned from the other three. Both trials for the same person stayed together. One development trial had already helped establish that the instrument could measure this target.

The model using both grids had a balanced error of 0.302°, compared with 0.271° for motion alone and 0.299° after swapping the electrodes. It beat both in 0 of four people. The electrical histories fell short of the preset test for added predictive information.

A simple forecast repeats the turn that the sensor just made over the preceding 100 milliseconds. Its balanced error was 0.315°.

What the forecast measures

A turn without a direction

A 5° turn to the left and a 5° turn to the right both score 5°. A sensor can turn then return to its starting orientation and score zero. Its readings describe the worn unit. Recovering a joint angle or a person’s position would require other measurements.

Why swap the electrodes?

Skin electrodes pick up a mixture of electrical sources. Muscles, the heartbeat and movement of the contacts can all contribute. For the recorded comparison we keep the person’s task and motion history fixed, then pair them with both grids from another time in the same trial, while the same person performed the same task. A forecast that survives the swap may need little information about the timing of those voltages.

How the model reads time

The Transformer splits the past 100 milliseconds into ten slices. In each slice it reads the sensor’s turn, its change since the start, the known task and the electrical summaries selected for that model. Two sets of learned attention weights let it compare earlier and later slices. It uses the last slice to estimate a correction to the turn already observed. Each choice of electrode grids has its own fitted model. Setting a grid’s signal size to zero gives that model a flat signal.

A different test of surface voltage

In an earlier study, people received a brief wrist stimulus. Adding an armpit electrode reading to a neck-voltage forecast did not pass the timing test on six new people. The walking study uses different participants, a different task and a different target. See the neck-voltage results.
