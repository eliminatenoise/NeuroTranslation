Can an early arm signal predict a later neck reading?

During a nerve study, people lay still. Electrodes at the armpit and neck picked up small voltages after each pulse at the wrist. Both used the same clock. By eight milliseconds, how much could the arm reading tell us about the neck?

Measured voltage · Eight events

What the neck electrode recorded

The first eight wrist pulses in one person’s third run gave a mean change of +1.70225 µV at the neck. Each event contributes equally.

This earlier check subtracted the neck average from −15 to −13 ms from its average at +12 to +14 ms. The model uses a longer baseline (−15 to −10 ms), so the two quantities can differ even on the same event.

The raw skin electrode picks up a mixture of electrical fields, including muscle, heart and stimulus-related activity. A positive voltage here does not identify the cells that produced it. The study recorded people lying still and supplies no movement target.

Recordings and method

Birgit Nierula and colleagues recorded these signals on one 10,000 Hz clock. The neck electrode sat over the sixth cervical vertebra and used a mid-back electrode as its reference. The source labels the armpit channel “Biceps” for its location. It records a surface electrical signal over the median nerve.

How the forecast works

A Transformer compares sixteen short intervals of electrical activity. Ten describe the pre-pulse baseline and six describe the early response. Each input interval keeps its place on the event clock. The model learns how strongly to weight the intervals together and returns one number in microvolts. Both versions omit pre-pulse armpit readings.

The target includes the negative of the baseline neck voltage, which also enters the model. A simple comparison predicts the average later voltage in the fitting group, then subtracts the current event’s early baseline. It shows how much forecasting is possible from that subtraction alone.
