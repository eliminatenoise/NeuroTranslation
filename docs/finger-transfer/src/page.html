What enters each estimate?

The Transformer learns which of four recent signal summaries to weigh together. Ridge learns a linear combination of those same summaries. The third predictor looks up the average movement for this word and instant in the training data. Only that table stays tied to the cue when you choose “Cortex only.” None of the three receives a glove reading at prediction time.

In the study, each summary came from 160 ms of voltage. The four windows ended 120, 80, 40 and 0 ms before the prediction. The slower band spans 12–32 Hz. The faster spans 70–150 Hz with 113.75–126.25 Hz omitted. Each contact’s band power was scaled against its own early recording, then averaged within its brain area.

Here the sliders set the last summary directly in units of that calibration spread. The four values rise or fall in equal steps from one quarter of the slider value to its full value. Other covered areas stay at their calibration mean. Toggling an area changes its model inputs after calibration. Unknown contacts have a presence flag and no named-area power value. Blank and display timing both start on an invented 40 ms grid boundary.

Recorded experiment · Stanford FingerFlex

Learn from four people. Test on a fifth.

Each person’s first 80 seconds of voltage and glove readings set their personal scales. After an eight-second gap, later trials from four people taught the models how to predict movement. The fifth person’s later trials supplied the test. Each person took that turn once.

Does the right recording help on this trial?

Swap in the preceding trial’s cortical signal from the same person, cued finger, phase and tick. Keep the true cue and clock. Using the correct signal lowered error for both models in all five people. Averaged over the five people, the cue table (0.4770) still had a smaller error than all eight model rows below. Two people went the other way: for D01 the ridge model with the correct cortex beat the cue table (0.4152 against 0.4315), and for D04 both cue-and-cortex models did (0.4993 and 0.4548 against 0.5454).

Can it tell two repetitions apart?

Four successive trials of the same cue form one group. Compare the change in movement between the second and fourth trials. The first and third supply the wrong signals for those trials. Across 56 such pairs, predicting identical movement on both tries beat the cue-plus-cortex prediction in every person, for both models.

Neither model passed the five-person transfer test. Before fitting, the study required gains in all five people and positive lower bounds for all six comparisons. Each person also needed at least six groups of four repeats across at least three cues. All five met that sample requirement. Each model passed only the comparison with a wrong recording on an individual trial.

Intervals describe the spread across all 3,125 ordered resamples of these five people, weighting each sampled person equally. Five clinical recordings and overlapping training sets leave population uncertainty unresolved.
