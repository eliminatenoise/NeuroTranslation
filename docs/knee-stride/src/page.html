How does the stride match work?

The walking files give 60 frames per second. Each forecast gets 240 pairs of signed right and left knee angles, oldest first. It compares the latest 60 pairs to 60 earlier pairs, shifting them back by 45 to 180 frames. The shift with the smallest sum of squared differences across both knees wins. A tie goes to the shorter shift. This estimated repeat interval can cover more than one physical stride.

The dot marks the frame that falls 15 frames past the earlier match. The simplest forecast copies those two angles. At nine points in the observed motion, the learned models compare both knees, the earlier bend and how quickly the angles changed over three frames. They add their correction to that same copied pair. The shared NeuroTranslate model instead summarizes the four seconds in four blocks. Three fitted corrections compare the block measurements alone, the same measurements with the frozen encoder, and a copy with its weights shuffled. The graph shows one forecast endpoint per knee.

What did the sensors measure?

Body-worn Xsens inertial sensors supplied the motion used to reconstruct these joint angles. Six of the 51 walks used HD whole-record processing. The other 45 carry the “Live” label. How soon each angle would be available to a live system remains unknown.

All walks here were on a treadmill at 0.5 m/s. The same 12 evaluation walkers had already been examined in an earlier study. Testing other walkers and speeds calls for fresh recordings. The study compares future angles in stored files. Electrical activity and control of a moving leg fall outside this comparison.
