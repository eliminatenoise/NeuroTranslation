Visual balance

When the view tilts,
where does the body go?

Walk along a narrow beam and the view through your headset suddenly tips. Your head and the bone at the base of your spine, the sacrum, are already moving. Can voltage recorded at the scalp help predict where they’ll be an eighth of a second later?

Does scalp voltage earn its place?

A changing forecast in the example tells you that an input matters to the model. Accuracy takes a separate test. Here it is the distance between a forecast and the optical marker actually measured 125 ms later.

On the historical holdout, neither marker showed a reliable advantage from adding matching scalp voltage under both pre-specified comparisons. The head forecast improved when the model got the matching scalp instead of another event’s scalp. The separately trained model without scalp still had the smaller mean error at both markers.

Each person counts equally after averaging their rotations. The three forecasts use the same events. The other-event control borrows scalp voltage from the same walker, rotation direction and third of the walk, with disjoint measurement windows. We resample whole participants 5,000 times to obtain the paired 95% intervals and one-sided 98.75% upper bounds adjusted for four comparisons. Six assessment people limit precision.

What the clock measures

Each model sees 160 stored samples, ending 125 to just under 129 ms after the logged visual rotation. Its target is 32 samples later. The archive runs at 256 Hz. Both landmarks are scored in three dimensions.

The authors filtered and re-referenced scalp, eye and neck voltages, and interpolated 100 Hz optical measurements onto the shared clock. A 125 ms lead in these processed files does not establish 125 ms of warning on a live device. The recordings cannot identify which brain signal caused a movement.

Follow the measurements

Steven M. Peterson and Daniel P. Ferris recorded the head and sacrum, scalp voltage, eye movements and neck and lower-leg muscle activity while participants walked on a treadmill-mounted balance beam. During the selected sessions, the headset view rotated 20° for half a second.
