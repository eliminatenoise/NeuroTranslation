One footstep, two forecasts

Can learning where the heel will move help a model predict how hard the floor will push back?

NeuroTranslation · Experimental research paper · 21 November 2026

Download PDF Download source and tables

Abstract

A camera tracks a marker on a walker’s right heel. A force plate measures the upward push beneath that foot. Stop the recording during contact, then advance it by a tenth of a second. The marker and the plate answer two different questions about the same step. We asked whether a model could learn each answer more accurately by learning both together.

We trained one sequence model, a Transformer, to read the preceding foot movement, the time since foot strike and surface muscle voltages. Two equally equipped specialist models learned one outcome apiece. A second experiment held an existing movement model fixed and varied how much information its two forecasts could share. We used 203 paired examples from 25 people for development, then scored both experiments on 53 examples from eight people and, separately, 31 from six of those same eight.

In the original assessment the shared model missed the heel by 1.156 mm and the force by 92.36 N, against 1.183 mm and 93.73 N for the specialists. The later walks also favoured sharing on both averages. All four uncertainty intervals for those differences included zero. In the fixed-encoder experiment, compressing the correction increased heel error in both assessments. Neither experiment established an advantage for the matched muscle recordings under its prespecified test.

What counts as an error? A heel forecast can miss by moving too far, too little or in the wrong direction. We measure the straight-line distance between its predicted and recorded endpoint in millimetres (mm). For the plate we measure the absolute difference between predicted and recorded upward force in newtons (N). Combining these numbers would hide which prediction improved.

Why follow the heel?

A walker can load a planted foot while the heel marker barely moves. Position and force can therefore share timing and still carry different information. The familiar act of stepping onto a plate gives that idea a measurable test. The study concerns barefoot, slow, level walking in recordings made by Céline Schreiber and Florent Moissenet. Putting the camera and plate on one clock

Cameras sampled two reflective markers 100 times a second, one at the back of the right heel and one over the second metatarsal near the ball of the foot. Electrodes on the skin sampled electrical activity from eight right-leg muscles 1,500 times a second. A force plate under the right foot sampled at that same higher rate. Recording muscle activity through skin electrodes is called surface electromyography (EMG). A newton measures force. The outcome here is the upward component in the laboratory coordinate system.

We subtracted the cutoff heel position from its future position along each laboratory axis, with Z pointing up. For each muscle we squared the voltage samples in each of four 60 ms bins, averaged them and took the square root. This root-mean-square value describes the size of a varying voltage. The last bin ended at the midpoint of the cutoff camera frame’s 15 analog samples. The future plate average covered +95⅓ through +104⅔ ms.

Which recordings supplied both answers?

The source study designated 0.4–0.8 metres per second as its slow C2 walking condition. We chose cutoffs 140, 240, 340 and 440 ms after the first annotated right foot strike. The original contact codes had to assign separate plates to the feet. Both markers had to lie over the assigned plate at strike and at the target, with its full force block before foot-off. Future geometry and foot-off determined eligibility. They were withheld from the inputs.

Two ways to share a prediction

1. Learn both answers in the same network

We divided the 24 camera frames into four ordered groups of six. Each group describes where the heel ended relative to the start of the history, where the forefoot sat relative to the heel, how far both markers moved, the final heel height and time since strike. Eight muscle-voltage summaries complete the group. A Transformer learns how much weight to give each group when it combines them. Two separate output layers read its final representation, one for the three heel coordinates and one for upward force.

Training gave equal weight to the two tasks after scaling the four target coordinates by their training variability. An error in either prediction could change the same 2,656 internal parameters. We checked this directly during fitting. The two outcomes produced nonzero gradients, the adjustments each error requested of the shared parameters, and the shared parameters actually changed. Each specialist had the same internal capacity, inputs, starting weights and training choices as the joint model. A pair of specialists needed two copies of its internal network.

2. Hold the movement model fixed and restrict the correction

Start with a simple guess. Let the heel repeat its last 100 ms of movement and let the plate show the typical training force at that time since strike. An existing movement encoder, a network that turns six marker changes per group into a 24-number summary of the history, stayed fixed. An output layer used that summary alongside the original marker and voltage features, strike timing and recent heel displacement to learn corrections to the guess.

In the rank-two version, all four correction coordinates had to come from the same two learned combinations of inputs. In the unrestricted version, each coordinate could use its own combination. Both fits penalized large coefficients, a method called ridge regression. This checks how much the correction can be compressed. The two learned numbers have no assigned anatomical meaning.

How we kept a walker’s repeated steps together

All windows and repeats from a person stayed in one development fold, a group temporarily left out of a fit. The end-to-end experiment used three outer folds, with inner training splits to choose the training duration. The fixed-encoder experiment used five folds. For each reported mean, we averaged windows within a walk, walks within a person, then people equally. We froze the final models fitted on development data and scored each historical recording set separately. The source archive accompanying this paper gives additional fit settings.

It missed the joint criterion in both sets.

What the floor can settle

In the original assessment the average heel miss was about a millimetre whether the model learned one task or two. That apparent precision belongs to a skin marker. The skin can move relative to the heel bone, and the study gives no error bound for each tiny displacement. A small change in forecast error therefore says little about which model tracks the underlying bone more faithfully.

The force plate does answer a different question about the same contact. Why might the muscle recordings add so little predictive information here? The marker and elapsed time already tell the model where the walker is in the step. Each skin electrode samples electrical activity in its vicinity. Its voltage alone cannot identify the force of an individual muscle or the pressure under a particular part of the sole. Swapping voltages tests their extra predictive information in this setup. A physiological test of how a particular muscle contributes to force would need a different measurement.

The original recordings were gap-filled and filtered before release. The marker and force filters used samples in both time directions. Muscle voltage was centred across the trial and filtered. A segment ending at our cutoff can therefore contain information from later raw samples. The source methods describe a 5 N threshold for force, while the linked processing code uses 50 N. We retained stored zeros, which can also reflect missing force. A future test of live prediction would need prospective foot-strike detection, one-direction processing, a sensor delay budget and a new group of walkers.

The same people supplied the two historical recording sets, and their roster had been used in earlier studies. Confining the test to steps that landed cleanly on one plate especially limits what we can conclude about different surfaces, speeds and unsuccessful contacts. Within this narrow task, two training targets did update one network. The original and later walks leave the size and even the direction of any accuracy advantage uncertain.
