How fast will the cylinder turn?

A gyroscope measures how fast an object turns. Can its accelerometer tell us something about the next tenth of a second that the gyroscope has missed?

Abstract

We used 200 ms of recorded motion to forecast the cylinder’s average angular velocity during the next 100 ms. Each forecast knew the hand and assigned target. Repeated movements by 24 people supplied the test. The full model missed the future angular-rate vector by 0.1584 rad/s on 2,653 matched windows. The gyro-plus-task model missed by 0.1622 rad/s. Their paired difference was −0.00378 [−0.00799, +0.00047] rad/s (95% interval). Borrowing another trial’s acceleration made forecasts worse, but the matched comparison with gyro plus task remained uncertain. In a separate silent-gesture game, the model given the object concept from an assigned word-and-picture prompt missed by 193.66 mm on repeat attempts. Earlier pose alone missed by 179.83 mm.

One object, one clock

The Ruhr experiment measured a 41 g object in two ways. Cameras followed its markers around the room. A sensor fastened to the object measured acceleration and turning on three axes. The cameras let an observer see where the object went. Our question requires knowing which readings were already available when a forecast began. The published fine alignment uses later movement, including its destination, to align the two paths. We therefore use the sensor’s own clock for the entire prediction. Sziburis et al., 2025.

Participants saw a target on a screen, waited for a sound, then carried the cylinder. Left and right hands had separate sessions. Of the original 31 people, we used the same 24 people selected for an earlier development study. Within each hand and target, assignments three to six fit the models, seven and eight supplied a development check, and nine and ten tested the fixed forecasts. The first two assignments and the other seven people fall outside this study. Random ordering across targets permits overlap in recording time between partitions. In 178 of 804 eligible test recordings, a later trial for a different target in the same hand session contributed to fitting. Each held repetition belongs to a familiar person performing a familiar task.

What the device exported

The MTw2 produces a row every 10 ms on the exported clock. The accelerometer reports m/s² including gravity. Holding the cylinder still while tipping it can change the readings along its axes. The gyroscope reports rad/s about those same axes. Both already undergo instrument processing. The files lack an independent measure of processing delay or packet arrival, and set no exact sensor-clock time for the visual cue or sound. We supplied the target from its recorded assignment. MTw manual, §§11.6–11.7.

Twenty readings in, ten readings ahead

Consider the 600 ms cutoff. Its input runs from 400 to 590 ms. The answer is the mean of readings taken at 600, 610, …, 690 ms, a three-component vector in the cylinder’s axes. We repeat the forecast at 200, 600, 1,000, 1,400, 1,800, 2,200 and 2,600 ms from the first instrument row. The 100 Hz grid had to match within 0.0000001 s. Missing, skipped or nonfinite rows could not be interpolated into a forecast.

Reading the history

Four consecutive samples make a 40 ms block. For each sensor we took the last and the mean XYZ reading in each block. Five blocks, their elapsed times, the hand and the hand-specific target entered a small Transformer. Its attention layer learned how much each earlier block mattered to the last one. Three fitted copies started from different random weights. Each predicted two three-component quantities, mean angular velocity and mean acceleration.

The network has 16 features per block, five learned positions, two attention heads and a 32-unit feed-forward layer. Each fit ran for 20 passes in batches of 256 with AdamW (learning rate 0.0015, weight decay 0.001, gradient cap 1). The two output errors carried equal weight after centring their training-axis means and scaling by a separate root-mean-square training deviation for each sensor. Standardised input features were clipped at ±10. An extra model learned only the angular-rate target. All normalisation and fitting used the training recordings. Linear comparisons used the same blocks, a free intercept and ridge penalty of 0.01. Persistence reused the last ten readings’ mean. The trend added that mean’s change from the preceding ten.

Two ways acceleration can earn its place

First, remove acceleration and train again with the same angular-velocity history and task assignment. Second, give the full model another recording’s acceleration. The substitute comes from the closest earlier eligible recording in the same partition with the same person, hand, target and cutoff. The recipient’s gyro and destination stay put. A donor must exist for both comparisons to use identical windows. Swapping a signal breaks its normal relation to the other reading, so this comparison measures sensitivity to the pairing.

We measured the length of the difference between predicted and observed three-axis vectors. A smaller miss is better. We averaged windows within recording, recordings within person, and people equally. We scored seeds 17, 41 and 73 separately before averaging errors. Each of 10,000 paired resamples drew whole people with replacement (seed 20261124). A benefit required the upper 95% percentile bound for both full-minus-control comparisons to fall below zero. The other comparisons describe this sample.

What the gyroscope already knew

On the same 379 held recordings, the full model’s error minus that of gyro plus task was −0.00378 [−0.00799, +0.00047] rad/s. Against substituted acceleration the difference was −0.01881 [−0.02583, −0.01262] rad/s (95% paired intervals). The two prespecified comparisons did not establish an incremental acceleration benefit on the donor-matched repetitions. With all 5,628 valid windows, the full-minus-gyro difference was −0.00440 [−0.00765, −0.00110] rad/s. That larger set includes recordings with no earlier donor, so it answers only the first comparison.

Does the projected turn agree with the instrument?

A rate of one radian per second, sustained for a tenth of a second, turns the sensor by 0.1 radian, or about 5.73°. We started from the orientation at the final input row, projected the predicted angular-rate vector for 0.1 s, and compared the endpoint with the device’s orientation estimate ten rows later. This asks how far apart two orientations are, in degrees.

The device supplies orientation as four numbers, a unit quaternion. For a sensor-to-local-Earth quaternion, the increment about the sensor axes multiplies on the right, giving q̂₁ = q₀ exp(0.1 ω̂ / 2), where products are quaternion products and ω̂ is treated as a pure quaternion. We normalised finite endpoint quaternions within 0.01 of unit norm. Error is the shortest angular separation, 2 arccos(|q̂₁ · q₁|), converted to degrees. Opposite quaternion signs represent the same orientation.

The instrument estimates orientation by combining sensor signals. Its output provides a physically interpretable recorded comparison, with a shared measurement source. The camera never supplies an independent angle here. Absolute heading against a target on the table would also require a calibration of the sensor frame to the task frame.

A second attempt at a silent gesture

In the separate NEMO–Lowlands study, a player saw an object word and picture, then improvised how to depict it for a robot. On the repeat trials here, the robot had guessed the first attempt incorrectly. The model received the assigned concept and early movement. The preceding mistake explains why the person gestured again. The model never saw the guess, the feedback text or the first movement. For the substituted-cue test, the same poses were paired with the next distinct training-known concept in alphabetical order within the same code and split, cycling at the end. de Wit, Krahmer & Vogt, 2021.

We marked movement when the same wrist remained ≥30 mm from its starting position relative to its shoulder for three successive frames. Five poses spanning the next 200 ms predicted the displacement from the last pose to the recorded pose nearest one second after confirmation. We fitted 283 first attempts from 65 archive codes across 33 objects. Of 83 repeat recordings in each later group, 80 per group matched the concept and game sequence. Four lacked usable pose timing in development, leaving 76. The reserved group retained 80 repeats from 22 codes and 31 objects. All 80 had a comparison cue and a documented incorrect earlier guess. Success on the second attempt was immaterial.

The original full model increased the miss by 13.83 mm against pose alone (paired 95% interval +4.05 to +24.65), and by 9.37 mm against the substituted cue (−5.14 to +24.43). The required joint benefit failed. Shrinking predictions toward zero by a factor selected from 0 to 1 in steps of 0.05 on three separate first-attempt training folds reduced the full error by 7.90 mm relative to staying still (1.78 to 14.32 mm smaller). The calibrated full model still missed by 3.68 mm more than calibrated pose alone (+1.13 to +6.57), and by 1.08 mm more than calibrated substituted cue (−2.49 to +5.03). Seven of 80 repeats moved both wrists less than 30 mm.

Intervals resample whole archive codes 10,000 times, keeping the wrists and repeats together. The same codes had appeared in earlier first-attempt analyses. An archive code does not establish a person’s identity. The word and picture came together, while the player chose the action. Neither the language contribution nor a causal change induced by the robot’s mistake can be isolated here.

Where the finding travels

Borrowing acceleration from another repetition disturbed the forecast more than removing acceleration and retraining. A model can learn to use a second signal even when a model trained without it predicts almost as well. On these repetitions, the sensor pair also worked best when the hand and clock were supplied without the target number. Giving the model a destination did not guarantee a better description of how the object turned along the way.

A future trial could send a common hardware event to the sensor and cameras, and measure their residual timing error before forecasting. With that clock, a forecast from the cylinder could finally be tested against a second instrument without using the movement’s ending to line them up. An untouched group of people and a prospective trial roster would test how much transfers beyond these familiar repetitions. Neither study recorded muscle or nerve activity, and offline forecasting gave the participants no new feedback.
