Where will the wrist be in a fifth of a second?

Download the illustrated PDF

A reflective dot on a walker’s wrist travels far enough in 200 milliseconds to make standing still a poor guess. How much do the last forty camera frames tell us about its next twenty? And what do electrodes on the leg add once we know how the body is moving?

We forecast the displacement of six dots, one on the outer ankle, outer knee and thumb side of the wrist on each side. Two markers at the front of the pelvis helped locate them. We compared a linear regression with a Transformer trained to correct the linear forecast. Forty stored frames spanned 390 ms. We grouped them into eight segments and supplied either the marker histories alone or those histories plus eight channels of surface muscle voltage from the right leg (EMG).

What changed when the walker turned around?

The ten-metre walkway works in both directions. In ten of the twenty later walks, the person travelled in the opposite direction to their preceding selected recording. A model that searches that earlier recording for a familiar movement can find the right part of a stride and still send the wrist the wrong way down the room.

Two tests fixed before fitting

Adding EMG to the Transformer lowered the mean error from 18.22 to 18.02 mm. Six of ten walkers improved. For the twenty later walks we also replaced the current muscle signal with one from the preceding selected recording. Across 815 cutoffs, the correct signal gave 18.05 mm against 18.97 mm for the substitute. The muscle claim required both comparisons to favour the correct signal, a paired 95% interval entirely below zero, and improvement in at least seven of ten walkers in each comparison. Only the substitution test passed.

The prespecified architecture test required the combined Transformer to beat both linear fits as well as the simpler controls. The linear marker model already missed by 17.78 mm. Adding a Transformer to these histories did not establish a smaller error.

One more pass down the walkway

The original same-person template in laboratory axes missed by 133.61 mm. After seeing the primary scores, we aligned each history to the direction of travel using earlier pelvis movement, then matched the three nearest histories in the preceding selected recording. Its error fell to 18.94 mm. The combined Transformer missed by 18.05 mm on the same cutoffs. Their paired interval, −4.25 to +1.98 mm, allows an advantage for either. This follow-up changes how we interpret the baseline. The original tests keep their results.

What if the dot has no name?

Hand the model nine positions from a single dot, one every 50 milliseconds. It could be on a heel, an elbow or the back of a hand. Can a rule learned at the ankles, knees and wrists follow that dot for the next 200 milliseconds?

This second experiment used only those six named sites for fitting. The model received the motion of one point at a time, without its name, the other markers or any muscle signal. We rotated horizontal changes into the point’s recent direction of travel when it had moved at least 1 mm horizontally, then returned the prediction to laboratory coordinates for scoring. A frozen NeuroTranslate encoder summarized four successive segments. A fitted linear readout used those features and the original movements to correct a recent-velocity forecast.

These are different inputs, sampling intervals and averages from the six-point experiment. The two tables answer different questions.

The trained encoder missed by 31.00 mm at the 46 new sites. A radial basis model compared each history to 64 example histories and missed by 28.73 mm. The paired difference was +2.28 mm (95% walker interval +1.85 to +2.77). Shuffling the encoder weights also reduced mean error. The encoder had to reduce mean error by at least 5% against every specified control, with paired 95% intervals below zero. Neither set of sites met that requirement.

An arrow has a direction. How far did the ankle go?

Halfway between two right-foot strikes, the outside of the right ankle moved an average of 56.9 mm forward, 9.7 mm right and 65.4 mm up over the next 200 ms. Those three numbers give a mean arrow 87.2 mm long. The straight-line distance between observed endpoints averaged 88.2 mm across the same 89 strides from 30 walkers.

For the arrow we averaged signed forward, left and upward changes. A leftward and a rightward movement can cancel. For distance we first took each pair’s straight-line separation, then averaged trials within walkers and walkers equally. A curved path could be longer than the line joining its ends. A pair had to finish within its stride. At 89% of the stride four walkers still contributed a right ankle pair. At 90% only two did, so the graph stops. This overlay uses the training walkers and describes recorded travel. It supplies no prediction error.

What can a camera establish?

Schreiber and Moissenet recorded barefoot, injury-free adults at a target 0.4–0.8 m/s on a level walkway. They reconstructed missing marker positions and filtered the 100 Hz signals forward and backward at 6 Hz. They also centred each 1,500 Hz muscle channel over the trial and filtered it in both directions. Stored samples can thus depend on later observations. The 200 ms lead belongs to the processed record. Live forecasting, joint-centre motion and neural activity each require other evidence.
