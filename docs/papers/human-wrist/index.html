How much does cortical voltage add to a wrist forecast on the next recording day?

Give a model half a second of arm movement and electrical activity. Then give it the same arm movement with the electrical activity from an earlier reach. Can it tell which history belongs to this wrist?

Abstract

We fitted models to 60 right-wrist movement events from one participant in AJILE12 and tested them on 58 events from the next recording. The camera supplied estimated positions of both wrists. We scored each forecast at eight and fifteen frames, about 267 and 500 milliseconds after the last observed frame. On the 57 movements with an earlier voltage donor, a Transformer with a bounded correction improved on pose alone at both horizons for each of three fits of the right wrist. It improved on borrowed voltage in three of those six comparisons. For the accompanying left wrist it improved on pose alone in three comparisons and on borrowed voltage in four.

Limiting the distance that a model could move the wrist from a simple starting forecast cut error substantially. Yet the lowest observed mean errors on the full test set came from adding the training-day mean displacement on the right (29.40 and 47.32 pixels), and from keeping the last known position on the left (45.59 and 51.79 pixels, tied with fitted velocity). This test supports a smaller correction to recent pose. It leaves the contribution of the electrical history specific to the current reach unresolved.

What the recordings measure

Peterson and colleagues recorded people undergoing clinical monitoring for epilepsy. A grid of electrodes on the brain surface measured local voltage, while a room camera recorded their movements. From the public archive we used the first 16 hours of session 3 and one prospectively designated test recording, session 4, for the same participant. The original investigators located right-wrist movement onsets retrospectively using the video, including motion after the onset. Knowing where to place the forecast in time is part of the information supplied to every method here. [1, 2]

From an annotation to a test block

We required the second around onset to lie within one two-minute block, with continuous coverage from permitted coarse activity annotations and room on every required recording clock. We retained one event per original block with at least 30 seconds between events. On session 3, 60 of the 81 annotated onsets passed. On session 4, we took the first twelve admissible onsets in each successive four-hour period. Of 196 annotations, 27 failed block duplication or spacing, four crossed block boundaries, and 107 exceeded the period quota. The six periods contributed 3, 7, 12, 12, 12 and 12 blocks. This samples the earlier eligible events in each period more heavily.

Each event contributes 250 earlier voltage samples from eight grid contacts at 500 Hz and 15 earlier video frames at 30 Hz. The right branch sees the right wrist, elbow and shoulder. The left branch sees the corresponding left landmarks during those same right-wrist events. Both see the same electrodes. We fitted four outputs per wrist, horizontal and vertical displacement to each of the two horizons.

Clocks, contacts and coordinates

Both files use midnight UTC on successive deidentified archive dates as the nominal origin. Pose and voltage start at zero on their respective clocks. We ended each input just before onset and counted the two targets from the final observed video frame. A second stored onset field agreed within 2 ms. Hardware synchronization error remains unknown. The grid used the same eight stored contact IDs, locations and recording order on both days. Two of the eight carried the authors’ bad-channel flag on each day. We retained them under the original fixed selection rule.

The targets are DeepLabCut estimates from monocular video. The archive documentation identifies the coordinates as pixels. A nominal metre field has no usable conversion. The streams contain no per-frame tracking confidence. All required inputs and all four endpoints were finite for each of the 60 training blocks and 58 test blocks. Finite coordinates can still miss the wrist.

How far can a forecast move the wrist?

Write p for the last known wrist position and y for its observed displacement to a later frame. In the direct model, a Transformer reads the earlier pose and voltage and predicts y in one step. Its output is centered and scaled using only the training-day displacement at that wrist, horizon and image axis. A new recording can present unfamiliar positions or electrical patterns. An unrestricted model can turn these into a very large displacement.

For the bounded forecast we first extend the wrist’s recent velocity. At horizon h, the extension v equals h times the difference between the last and first of the 15 earlier wrist frames, divided by 14 frame intervals. A fitted fraction a between zero and one sets how much of that extension to keep. The Transformer learns a correction for each image axis, scaled by the training-day standard deviation s of that coordinate’s displacement (floor 1 pixel).

Bounded endpoint = p + a v + 0.25 s tanh(z), where z is the learned output.
Each correction can move a coordinate by at most one quarter of its training-day scale.

For each wrist and horizon we selected a on session 3 by minimizing Σ ‖y − a v‖² + λ(a − 0.5)², with λ = 16 × max(median ‖v‖², 1 pixel²). To separate this fitted velocity term from the effect of limiting the correction, we also trained the identical bounded model with zero starting displacement. Both bounded models added the mean squared standardized correction to the mean squared standardized prediction error, with equal weight. The direct model used the latter error alone.

What the network receives

Five successive 100-ms bins each contain 26 features. Four describe the wrist and elbow relative to the shoulder. Six give the change of all three landmarks since the first earlier frame. For each of eight contacts we used the mean voltage and the natural log of its root-mean-square voltage, flooring the latter amplitude at 10⁻⁶ µV before taking the logarithm. Training inputs set the mean and population standard deviation of each feature. We used a divisor of one when that deviation was below 10⁻⁸.

One causal attention block of width eight combines the bins in time order. A width-twelve feedforward layer with tanh activation, root-mean-square normalization and a final-token readout complete the network. We trained separate wrists, and kept the same initialization, masks and step count in each comparison. The pose-only network received zeros in every standardized voltage feature. All fits used full-batch AdamW for 48 steps, rate 0.003, decay 0.02, β₁ = 0.9, β₂ = 0.999 and ε = 10⁻⁸, with double precision and no dropout or early stopping. Seeds 17, 37 and 73 remain separate throughout.

The simplest forecasts led on the new day

For each forecast we measured the straight-line image distance from its endpoint to the tracked wrist. Adding the mean displacement learned from the earlier 60 movements gave the smallest mean at both horizons on the right. The left wrist’s last position gave the smallest means on that side, equaled by the fitted velocity forecast and the duplicate geometric persistence control. The left-side median error for staying put was only 5.62 pixels at eight frames and 7.01 at fifteen. A few large distances can matter greatly to the mean, especially when the wrist usually moves little or a tracker wanders.

The correction bound carried most of the gain

The bounded models made much smaller errors than the direct Transformer across every seed, horizon and wrist in the voltage-informed fits. For example, seed 17 put the right wrist 130.16 pixels from its tracked position at fifteen frames with the direct model and 48.83 with the bounded correction to a fitted velocity. Removing the velocity term while retaining the bound and its training penalty gave 48.93 pixels. Most of the improvement survived that removal.

The fitted velocity behaved much like staying put. Its full-set mean errors matched persistence on the left, matched it at eight frames on the right, and differed by 0.054 pixels at fifteen frames on the right. The bounded model’s direct comparison with the unrestricted model therefore tests a change in output range and training penalty as well as the addition of the kinematic starting point. The resulting difference cannot be assigned to a detailed law of arm motion.

What geometry alone bought

A separate model decomposed wrist movement into shoulder translation, elbow motion relative to shoulder, and wrist motion relative to elbow. For each component it combined slopes over the last 4, 8 and 15 camera frames, using nonnegative weights whose sum per component could reach one. Joint-component and whole-wrist mixtures fitted both arms together, giving each event equal total weight. Another mixture fitted each arm separately. The shared joint-component mixture’s right-wrist error was 32.02 and 57.56 pixels. It missed the prespecified test of beating both straight velocity and the whole-wrist mixture at both horizons. The image-plane decomposition respected the relative positions of the joints. On this test, fitting the extra slopes increased forecast error.

Whose voltage was it?

The sharpest test holds the forecaster and the arm still and swaps only the electrical history. We supplied the most recent eligible voltage segment from a strictly earlier selected block on the same day, retaining its natural time course. The recipient kept its own pose, starting wrist position and observed future. The donor was chosen before outcomes were opened. That left 57 distinct blocks with all four outcomes, all three forecasts and both wrists present. The first block had no eligible predecessor.

For each block we subtracted the control’s Euclidean endpoint error from the error of the bounded, fitted-prior model using actual voltage. The tables report the mean paired difference. A negative value favours the actual voltage. W / T / L counts blocks with smaller, equal or larger error respectively, using a 10⁻⁹-pixel tie tolerance. Comparing the 58-block mean for one model with a 57-block donor mean would answer a different question.

On the annotated side, all six paired means favoured actual voltage over pose alone. One advantage, seed 37 at fifteen frames, was less than one ten-thousandth of a pixel. Only three of the six also favoured actual over borrowed voltage. The left wrist failed the same prespecified requirement of a lower mean than both controls at every seed and horizon. Restricting the analysis to blocks complete across both wrists and both horizons returned exactly these same 57 observations and differences.

The sign depended on the recording period

Each training seed saw the same held movements. Splitting those movements by time asks a different question from repeating a fit. The six periods contributed 2, 7, 12, 12, 12 and 12 donor-matched blocks. The shorter first two samples make their period-specific means particularly sensitive to an individual large error.

Weighting the six periods equally left only four of the six right-wrist pose comparisons in favour of voltage. For seed 37 at fifteen frames, the block-weighted difference of −0.000081 pixels became +0.239 pixels. For seed 73 at that horizon, −0.030 became +0.177. Omitting one period at a time preserved a smaller right-wrist error in every omission for four of six pose comparisons and just one of six donor comparisons.

These are sensitivity checks on one held recording. The six periods are unevenly sampled parts of one day, and the three seeds repeat a fitting procedure. Neither supplies independent participants from which to estimate a population interval. A donor from the same recording also shares setting, electrode hardware and potentially slow changes in brain state. A model can use features common to both electrical segments without identifying which reach they belong to.

More training events or a different model?

The earlier right-wrist model learned from 16 movements. To separate the effect of training coverage from the change in architecture, we repeated every new right-wrist fitting recipe on those exact 16 events, refitting its feature scales, output scales and velocity prior. Then we ran the same recipe on 60, holding initialization, optimization and test windows fixed. This comparison concerns these particular additional events. They also extend the range of training times.

The 60-row bounded, fitted-prior forecast improved on its 16-row counterpart at both horizons for all three seeds. Expanding the direct fit helped all three at fifteen frames but increased eight-frame error for seeds 17 and 37. Even the largest mean error of the three bounded, fitted-prior 16-event models (31.91 and 53.04 pixels) was lower at each horizon than the smallest mean error of the three 60-event direct models (57.78 and 81.84 pixels). The 16-event study covered the right wrist only.

The earlier test stayed inside session 3

A separate earlier experiment used those same 16 fit events, with eight more for development. Of 24 preselected blocks in a later four-hour interval of that recording, 23 had usable inputs and targets. Continuing the recent right-wrist velocity had the lowest observed mean errors there, 15.58 and 38.35 pixels. Giving voltage to the direct Transformer increased its error relative to pose alone on every seed and horizon on that later-time test. The historical direct weights were reproduced by the right-only 16-event direct fit and were also scored separately on session 4. The earlier residual model added an unrestricted correction to half velocity. At fifteen frames on the same-recording test, its three voltage-informed fits missed by 74.81, 80.16 and 51.22 pixels on average. Those large errors motivated limiting the correction. Changes in sampling, movement and camera view limit any comparison of absolute errors across the two tests.

What transfers across a night?

A model can benefit from a limit on how much movement it predicts even when the input that interests us most supplies little verifiable information about the current reach. Training on more events improved the bounded right-wrist forecast here. The addition of voltage looked favourable against the same network trained on pose alone. Once the network had to distinguish the actual voltage from a plausible earlier history, that apparent advantage depended on which fit and which horizon we examined.

The original archive spans many people and days. This experiment holds out one recording of one participant undergoing clinical care, with electrodes placed for clinical reasons. Stored contact coordinates and channel labels link the recordings, but sensitivity, reference conditions and gain may change. We fixed all fits and rules before releasing the earlier test histories. A second implementation reconstructed all predictions before the test targets were released. Independently computed aggregate errors agreed within 3 × 10⁻¹³ pixels. The original voltage was filtered and rereferenced offline. Clock correspondence and a causal network mask cannot establish end-to-end live latency. Change of viewpoint, zoom, occlusion and detector errors can all alter image displacement. Without calibrated depth, the errors give no metric reach distance, joint angle, force or muscle activation. The tracked endpoints cannot distinguish these effects from genuine variation in movement.

The annotations identify the right wrist with the benefit of later video. This design leaves open the harder questions of detecting an onset as it happens, tracking an independently initiated left movement, and transferring across participants. A stronger claim about the unique electrical contribution would require the same paired advantage against both controls on independently held recordings, with limits on tracking and timing error tight enough to resolve the size of the effect.

Data, implementation and rights

Original acquisition, pose estimates and annotations are by Peterson and colleagues. We selected windows and contacts, fitted forecasts, and drew every figure anew from group summaries. The downloadable source contains full-precision aggregate results for all 110 forecast configurations and 172 paired comparisons, and a local reading copy. It includes the publication builder, figure code, metric definitions and reuse terms. The PDF carries the same ZIP attachment. The public models use illustrative inputs and group summaries. Individual clinical traces, positions and fitted participant weights stay outside this release.
