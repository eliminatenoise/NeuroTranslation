Can a sensory brain signal improve a reach forecast?

A macaque holds a robot handle. First the robot nudges its hand. Later a target appears and the animal reaches for it. Pause the recording at either event. How far, and in which direction, will the handle move in the next tenth of a second?

The handle already tells us quite a lot. If it has just moved to the right, it may keep moving to the right. The target on the screen gives another clue. We asked what a recording from a touch- and movement-sensitive region of the brain, area 2, could add once the forecast already knew the recent path and the cues visible to the animal.

On later trials from this animal, the handle-and-cue model missed by 0.497 cm on average. Adding the cortical spikes gave 0.604 cm. The paired difference was −0.107 [−0.145, −0.076] cm (95% interval), so the interval supports a larger error with spikes.

Two questions in one recording

Chowdhury, Glaser and Miller asked how well the motion of the arm explained the firing of individual area 2 neurons. Their work showed that whole-arm movement could explain features of firing that the hand alone missed. Here we turn the question around. Can past firing improve an estimate of the future motion of an independently measured handle? A region can carry information about movement even if some other observation already supplies that information to the forecaster. Original study

A second comparison adds camera-tracked positions on the hand and forearm. It keeps the same handle target. This tells us how much the answer depends on how much of the moving arm we already see.

Recording and target

One arm, two ways to start moving

On 12 September 2017 macaque C held a handle that could move across a horizontal plane. In the original experiment a motor could give the hand a brief push during the center hold. The animal recovered the center position, waited for a target and then reached toward it. The archive for this day contains 638 original trials. All 540 rewarded trials carry both a bump time and a later go time. The two events share an animal, an arm and an original trial. Dataset

Encoders on the robot’s joints measured the handle position in two directions, stored in centimetres. An array in the arm representation of area 2 recorded electrical signals. The original experiment sampled these at 30,000 samples per second and sorted detected spikes by their waveforms. The archive gives a count every 10 milliseconds (ms). Of 82 count columns, 30 have a positive sorting identifier and 52 have identifier zero. We use those 30 sorted columns as putative units for this session. A sorting label cannot identify the same neuron on another day.

Look ahead 100 msTwo handle positions, one change

Each prediction has two numbers. For example, a change of (+1, −0.5) cm would put the handle one centimetre farther along x and half a centimetre back along y. We subtract the position at the forecast time from the position ten stored rows later. The error is the straight-line distance between the predicted and recorded endpoints, in centimetres. Signed errors can cancel across trials. Distance keeps a miss to the left from cancelling a miss to the right.

We placed forecasts 0, 50, 100 and 150 ms after each recorded event where eligible. A bump forecast and its outcome had to precede the reach cues. A cue entered the past observations only once its timestamp lay strictly before the relevant bin endpoint. At an event boundary its direction can still be unavailable. In the fixed illustration, original 499, the push occurred 1.63 s after trial start. The target and go cue followed at 1.94 s. Both recorded directions were 180°. Anchoring forecasts to these events leaves actual hand-movement onset free to vary. The recording contains no speech, written instruction or natural-language annotation.

Planar handle · Forecast error

What does the right neural history buy?

Three fits started with different random weights. For each, we trained the same small Transformer, which can learn to weight different parts of a recent history, using different combinations of handle motion, spikes and cues. The primary comparison adds spikes to a model that already knows the recent handle motion and observed cues. The second keeps the trained joint model and gives it spikes from a different, earlier trial with matching task conditions. A useful spike pattern specific to the current trial should survive both comparisons.

On validation, the paired advantage from adding spikes was −0.104 [−0.139, −0.063] cm. The later-trial wrong-minus-correct difference was −0.010 [−0.058, 0.030] cm. With the other observations fixed, the interval does not resolve an advantage over the other trial’s spikes. Positive differences favor the joint model with the correct spikes.

On matched later trials the joint model’s mean absolute x and y errors were 0.423 and 0.362 cm. Its signed biases (forecast minus recording) were −0.045 and −0.017 cm. Including windows without donors, the joint and motion-plus-cue errors were 0.548 and 0.462 cm.

In the linear model, pooling spikes across neurons in each interval gave 0.493 cm, averaging each neuron’s firing across time gave 0.503 cm, and swapping the full spike history gave 0.540 cm. The Transformer added-spike differences at seeds 17, 41 and 73 were −0.064, −0.141 and −0.116 cm.

Two camera landmarks · Separate linear comparison

What if the forecast can see more of the arm?

The camera tracked points painted on the limb. We used the third hand marker, Marker_3, and Pronation_Pt1, a point on the ulna near the elbow. Each contributes a three-dimensional location relative to the shoulder and a recent change, in metres. The robot still supplies the two-dimensional answer in centimetres. The elbow-adjacent point is a proxy for that part of the forearm. It does not locate the upper arm or the elbow joint center exactly.

Here a regularized linear model learns a correction to a simple mechanical guess, continuing the last 40 ms of handle motion. We compare the same handle-and-cue inputs with and without the two markers, then ask what the spikes add after the markers. Fits using only markers or only spikes predict the whole change directly. The camera positions underwent alignment, filtering and resampling. The pipeline can interpolate missing camera observations. The archive omits original images and per-frame confidence. A finite stored position can therefore be an estimate between observations. OpenSim, the original authors’ musculoskeletal model, also estimates joint angles and muscle-path lengths. Neither quantity enters these fits. No direct muscle excitation or tendon-strain measurement was available.

Methods and interpretation

What the clock lets us claim

Separation across time. Of the first 324 rewarded originals assigned to fitting, 322 contributed 2,576 eligible windows. Originals 119 and 329 displayed the target before the bump and were excluded from both events. The next 108 supplied validation, and the final 108 were sealed until the analysis was fixed. Both events and every overlapping window from one original stayed together. Weights, scaling, and comparison rules were fixed before inspecting later outcomes. The demonstration trial, original 499, was fixed as the first original in the later block before its errors were known.

What went in. Each of five steps can contain the recent x/y endpoint, the preceding 40-ms x/y change, spike counts per unit, and seven values identifying already observed cues and directions. Neither a trial number, a future target direction, an archived velocity nor a reward entered the network. For each fit, all 41 input slots were retained; missing modalities were masked to zero after training-only standardization. Models trained for 24 passes over the fitting data, using seeds 17, 41 and 73. Validation selected no checkpoint or seed. It estimates performance later in this one session.

Controls. Zero displacement assumes the handle stays put. The three velocity forecasts prolong its last 40, 100 or 200 ms of motion. Ridge regression fits a weighted linear combination of the histories with a penalty on large weights. Additional linear controls retain a total count across neurons in each interval, or average each unit across all intervals. The wrong-trial test changes the neural history alone while holding movement, cues, event and time since event fixed. It preserves visible task conditions while disrupting the identity of the trial that produced the spikes. Any effect can reflect trial-specific firing differences beyond timing alone.

How much evidence? We resampled ordered blocks of neighboring trials to retain dependence between nearby reaches. Both named handle contrasts use 5,000 circular draws of eight rewarded trials per block, 95% percentile intervals and fixed predictions. The planned test of added spike information requires an interval above zero for the motion-plus-cue error minus the joint-model error. Evidence for a trial-specific advantage also requires an interval above zero for wrong-trial error minus correctly paired error. Direction and individual seed results are descriptive.

Why an online test is a different experiment. Preceding stored rows are available to this analysis, but the documented encoder processing uses forward and backward filtering. Camera processing also uses neighboring frames. Exact per-sensor delays and the precise preprocessing revision for this session are unknown. An online test would need time-stamped observations available at prediction time, a fresh session and a separately fixed model. These data alone cannot identify a causal neural command, decode language, or demonstrate a working control implant.
