NeuroTranslation · Human movement

Can a wrist forecast travel from one person to another?

Freeze a video just before a reach. Mark the wrist’s last position. How far from that mark will the wrist be half a second later?

Abstract

We fitted a movement forecast on 60 archived reaches from one person. We then gave it the half-second of camera-derived arm positions before each of 30 reaches from a different person. The reach-defining wrist switched from right to left between people. We used the earlier shoulders to put the two sides into a common picture, made the forecasts and compared them with the wrist positions in two later frames.

The primary forecast, a regularized linear regression, missed the reach-defining wrist by an average of 35.77 pixels at eight frames and 94.32 pixels at fifteen. A simpler forecast that continued a fraction of the wrist’s earlier velocity missed by 17.11 and 44.18 pixels. The regression lost at both horizons. A separate two-arm Transformer gained 0.14 pixel on average over its blocked-attention counterpart across both wrists and horizons. When full and matched fits were compared on the same 29 reaches with an earlier partner, the intact-minus-blocked difference changed from −0.073 to +0.047 pixel.

A camera-based wrist estimate supplies a point in an image. Here the archive already tells us when a movement began. Detecting an upcoming reach in continuous time, locating it in a room and reading an intention from the brain are different experiments. This one asks how accurately a camera-based forecast travels to a second person.

Methods · Recordings and selection

Which wrist moved?

AJILE12 records spontaneous activity during clinical epilepsy monitoring. Separate pose detectors trained for each person estimated nine upper-body landmarks from one camera view at nominally 30 frames per second. We used the wrists, elbows and shoulders. The source authors obtained written consent and institutional review board approval. Their annotations identify a movement of the wrist opposite the implant. No spoken reach instruction is attached to these events. [1–3]

We chose the lowest numbered available session for the next distinct participant in the frozen release inventory, before inspecting that person’s trajectories. Both recordings happen to be session 3. The first 16 hours of the source person’s recording contained 81 annotated starts. Sixty met the frozen selection rules. The remaining 21 shared a retained two-minute block or were too close to a retained start.

The second person’s day contained 51 annotated starts. Working forward in time, we kept up to 12 per four-hour period, one per original 120-second block and at least 30 seconds apart. The half-second on either side had to stay within its block and the permitted activity labels had to cover that interval. Labels included talking, watching TV, computer or phone use, eating, other activity, active and inactive. Sixteen starts failed spacing or shared a block, one crossed a block boundary and four exceeded the quota. These tests ran in a fixed order. Thirty starts remained in 30 distinct blocks.

The last frame determines the clock

For an onset t seconds into the archived day, let j be 30t rounded up in decimal arithmetic. The input frames run from j − 15 through j − 1 inclusive. All precede the annotated onset. The wrist outcomes at j + 7 and j + 14 lie 8 and 15 frames after the last input. The archived event and reach-table times agreed within 0.002 s. A separate 500 Hz electrical clock was indexed for registration. No electrical sample entered a forecast.

Methods · Camera geometry and fitted controls

A ruler that travels with the shoulders

The same wrist movement can cover more pixels when someone sits closer to a camera. For each event we drew a line from the implant-side shoulder to the opposite shoulder in each earlier frame. The median length of those 15 lines supplied a ruler, one projected shoulder width. The direction of their mean vector set the sideways axis. We turned that axis through a right angle and chose the sign pointing up the image. The midpoint of the two shoulders in the last earlier frame supplied the origin. Both later endpoints were evaluated in that fixed frame.

We read the landmarks by their anatomical names and ordered each arm wrist, elbow, shoulder. This puts opposite native wrists in the same role and accommodates a horizontal reflection, a constant image translation and uniform scaling within the admitted range. Camera motion, turn of the torso and foreshortening can still change the apparent distance. The archive’s position field says metres, yet its conversion is missing and the methods describe pixels. There is no verified conversion to centimetres or depth. [1,4]

Fit and score each wrist at each horizon

Each of four regressions received the 15 earlier frames of its arm in shoulder widths. We subtracted its last wrist position from the wrist, elbow and shoulder in every frame, giving 90 ordered numbers. From these the model predicted the two-coordinate displacement to the future wrist. On the 60 source rows for each output, we centred each feature and divided by its population standard deviation (values below 10−8 were set to one). We minimized mean squared vector error plus the sum of squared slopes, with unit penalty and an unpenalized intercept. This is ridge regression.

Holding the last position supplies a zero-displacement prediction. Constant velocity advances the wrist by its first-to-last displacement multiplied by 8/14 or 15/14. The damped version multiplies this by a gain between zero and one chosen to minimize squared displacement error on the source rows. All four gains were fixed before testing. On the accompanying wrist the resulting forecasts coincided with holding position. There are 14 frame intervals between the first and last input.

We required finite shoulders throughout, width ≥ 1 px, length of the mean shoulder vector ≥ half that width, and an absolute vertical component ≥ 0.10 for the perpendicular unit axis. Each forecast also required finite prior wrist, elbow and shoulder for its own arm. An unavailable input withheld that prediction. Missing outcomes would exclude only the relevant scores. All 30 test events had usable histories and both later endpoints for both wrists. No event was replaced.

Results · Primary comparison

Holding position was hard to beat

We measured the straight-line miss between the forecast and the detector’s later wrist position. For the registered comparison, we divided each miss by that event’s earlier shoulder width, subtracted the damped-velocity error from the ridge error on the same reach, and averaged. Improvement required a negative mean on the reach-defining wrist at both horizons. The observed differences were +0.157 and +0.411 shoulder widths, or +18.66 and +50.14 detector pixels.

A four-hour period containing one reach gives little evidence about that time of day. Clock periods cannot isolate what the participant was doing, differences in pose quality or what caused an error.

Two people, two image scales

On the 60 source reaches the earlier shoulder width ranged from 69.74 to 133.62 px, with a median of 103.96. On the 30 test reaches it ranged from 73.09 to 156.60 px, with a median of 113.41. One test width lay beyond the source range. All thirty stayed in the analysis. A wider image measurement might reflect distance, posture, perspective or a detector error. Those causes cannot be separated here.

We fixed the four regressions, their feature scaling, the control gains, the two-arm model, its masks and its empirical error radii before releasing the held histories. We then stored the predictions and availability flags before the held wrist endpoints were released for scoring. A separate calculation checked the 16 forecasts, 21 paired comparisons, all six time strata and the source calibration ranks. This sequence protects the prospective test even though the original event annotations themselves were made retrospectively.

Secondary experiment · Both arms

Does the other arm help?

To forecast one wrist, a two-arm Transformer could consult the positions of the opposite wrist, elbow and shoulder. A second model of equal size blocked every attention connection across arms. Both still saw a common frame built from both shoulders. Their difference tests the value of the other arm’s time-varying input after alignment. It cannot separate physical coordination from shared viewpoint or tracking error.

In each prior frame the model represented each arm by four planar vectors: wrist from elbow, elbow from shoulder, shoulder from its final input position and wrist from its final input position. It divided them by the arm’s median projected upper-arm plus forearm length (required > 10−6 px). Thirty tokens, one per arm per frame, entered a single attention block of width 12, with two six-dimensional heads and a 24-unit tanh feed-forward layer. Learned time and arm embeddings, a cross-arm attention bias and a parameter-shared decoder let each final arm token correct its source mean displacement. A sigmoid gate and a tanh bound limited each component correction to half an earlier arm length.

Training used full batches, three initializations (17, 43, 97), a normalized pseudo-Huber loss (scale 0.1), AdamW, learning rate 0.003, weight decay 0.01, 60 updates and zero dropout. We averaged predictions across seeds. The full intact and blocked refits used all 60 source events. Matching the training rows to a control needing an earlier arm gave 59. In three source-development folds, prefixes of 24, 36 and 48 were followed by 12 rows each. Eleven per fold had an eligible earlier partner. Intact-minus-blocked errors were +0.53, −0.72 and +1.32 px. The prespecified cross-arm gain failed in development.

Results · Uncertainty on the second person

How often did a wrist land inside the circle?

Draw a circle around each predicted point. Give the model half a shoulder width of room and a miss of one full width falls outside. We chose the actual circle sizes before seeing the test person. The radii came from 33 distinct blocks on the source person, each forecast by an ensemble trained only on its earlier chronological prefix. For each model, wrist and horizon, sort the misses in earlier shoulder widths. For a nominal fraction p, take rank min(n, ceil((n+1)p)). With 33 blocks the 50%, 80% and 90% levels select ranks 17, 28 and 31.

Each test circle used its frozen radius times that reach’s earlier shoulder width. All 30 reaches had four endpoints. At the nominal 90% level, 28/30 selected wrists were inside the intact circles at each horizon, versus 22/30 and 24/30 accompanying wrists. The intact model enclosed all four endpoints on 1/30, 9/30 and 18/30 reaches at the three levels. The blocked model did so on 1/30, 8/30 and 17/30. The 90% label belongs to each source-based marginal radius. It assigns no 90% target to that four-at-once count.

These results describe where endpoints fell in a different recording. Repeated movements from one source person cannot establish exchangeability with another person. The models, their errors and their radii also share source data. Counting four endpoints per reach would give a misleading impression of the evidence for population coverage.

Discussion · What would test generalization?

More people change the question

Sixty reaches gave the forecaster sixty examples of one person. Thirty further reaches gave us thirty chances to watch it fail or succeed on one other person. Another thousand frames from either would improve the description of those recordings. They would add no new person to the test. Which differences mattered here remains open: the participant, implant side, movement mix, detector and camera geometry changed together.

A distributed follow-on study should enrol by person and recording site. Before opening outcomes, inventory independent recordings with bilateral landmarks, verified wrist identities, a comparable movement definition, documented frame rate and rights for the intended use. Recruit across both onset sides, sessions, viewpoints, activity types and contributing sites. Report recruitment and exclusions within those groups. Keep observations local where terms require it and return the same predefined aggregate statistics from each site.

Put all days from a person on one side of each split. Use training people for fitting, disjoint people for choosing settings and radii, and untouched people from a reserved site for the final test. Freeze selection and geometry before opening held endpoints. Compare ridge, persistence, damped velocity and the intact and blocked two-arm models on identical events. Report performance for each held person, give every person equal weight in the across-person summary, and show coverage and missingness by site, side and camera setup. Quantify uncertainty across independent people and sites when the enrollment supports it. This is a proposed study.

The original recordings all come from one hospital. Independent sites would need comparable data collection beyond this archive. The camera records projected movement. Depth, force, pain, handedness, movement intention and sensorimotor feedback were unmeasured. Different electrode layouts would require anatomical registration and a separate matched experiment before any neural-transfer question could be scored. Continuous onset detection would also need times when no reach occurred, a causal trigger and its false-alarm and delay measurements. For this movement forecast the conclusion is narrower: on the second person’s selected reaches, the registered ridge model lost to its damped-velocity control.
