Does the scalp tell us where the arm will go?

@eliminatenoise · November 2026

Tell someone to bend their elbow and you already know something about where their arm will go. Read the arm itself and you know a little more about this particular attempt. What does a recording from six scalp electrodes add?

Abstract

We compared forecasts of changes in three instruments on a supported right arm. Both models knew the visual instruction and could see the first quarter-second after the cue. In 210 trials from five people, adding scalp recordings to a task-trained Transformer changed its mean standardized error from 0.508530 to 0.508563. A second model made a small improvement when given the scalp, but a recording from a different repetition of the same instruction gave nearly the same result. Neither model met its prespecified test for added scalp information.

In the seven fitting participants, central scalp and eye recordings resembled each other more on the same trial than on a different repetition. The resemblance was already present before the cue. A trial can have a recognizable electrical pattern even when that pattern offers little help in forecasting movement.

What the instruments saw

Ofner and colleagues asked seated participants to bend or straighten the elbow, turn the forearm in either direction, open or close the hand, or rest. Their study examined how well electrical recordings distinguished movements. Here the instruction is given to the model. The unknown is how far each instrument reading will change. A supporting exoskeleton supplied the elbow and forearm-rotation channels. A glove supplied the channel at the base of the index finger. Original experiment

Forecasting the nine changes2 / 4

How much does matching the scalp to the arm buy?

Seven people supplied 882 fitting trials across 21 runs. Development held out one fitting person at a time. We froze the final fits, scoring rules and comparison criteria before opening five fourth runs, one from each assessment person. Each run contained six repeats of all seven cues. These five people had supplied earlier runs to a separate mechanical study. Their fourth runs were fresh, and none of their data entered these fits or their normalization.

The task-trained Transformer represented four time bins and a summary of the cue and latest arm levels with five width-eight tokens. Two attention heads learned how to combine them. Each model trained for 48 updates on the same fitting rows. The scalp channels were FC3, FC4, C3, C4, CP3 and CP4, each expressed relative to its first bin. Keeping the architecture fixed let us change which signals it received.

The scalp and history model improved on no change (0.564891) in all five people. Yet a cue alone scored 0.492784, using the training-weighted median change for each instruction and output. The scalp model beat the history-only Transformer in three people and lost in two. Its average difference was +0.000033 (paired 95% interval −0.001048 to +0.001375).

Giving each trial each of the five other scalp recordings from its own person, run and cue in turn raised error from 0.508563 to 0.508773. A model trained on mismatched repetitions and tested with mismatches scored 0.508821. Scalp and eye channels together scored 0.508763, against 0.509654 with eyes, cue and arm alone. Three bilateral scalp differences (0.507834) also missed the rule against three eye channels.

A formal comparison required at least 5% lower average error and an improvement in all five people. The scalp model cleared that threshold against no change alone. The combined tests for practical advantage and an increment beyond measured eyes both failed. Protocol, weights and all 13 contrasts

Correcting the expected movement3 / 4

What if we start with the typical response?

A second analysis began with nine typical changes for each cue. It took a median in each fitting run, then across runs within each person, then across people. That nested median scored 0.496962. The first study’s 0.492784 used a single coordinatewise weighted median of fitting trials per cue, with balanced weights and the midpoint at a central tie. Taking medians in stages can give a different answer even for the same recordings.

We kept the earlier movement encoder fixed and fitted corrections to its cue template with a loss that limited the influence of large errors. The input described motion across four bins without absolute starting levels. Each electrical signal was centered on its first two bins. A separate training-only regression estimated the part of each scalp channel predictable from all three eye channels. The joint forecast received arm readings, eye readings and the remaining scalp signal.

Reading a scalp–eye match4 / 4

The eyes and scalp shared a pattern before the cue

We averaged C3 and C4 at each sample and paired that trace with eye channel 63. In each 128-sample window we measured Pearson’s correlation, r, once with the simultaneous eye trace and once with the eye trace from the next repeat of that cue. Both windows exclude the cue tick. All 882 pairs from seven fitting people had finite, varying traces. Outer eye channels 62 and 64 swapped left/right labels in three runs, so all ocular comparisons keep the acquisition numbers.

In the quarter-second after the cue, the same-trial median |r| was 0.327 against 0.195 on another repetition. Before the cue these values were 0.330 and 0.202. We took the median magnitude within each run, across runs in each person, then across people. Figure 2 squares each trial’s correlation before averaging. Squaring 0.327 would therefore answer a different question. The optional variability screen retained 846 pairs and preserved the same direction.

What the comparison can tell us

All seven fitting people showed stronger same-trial coupling in both windows. A single electrode can pick up variation shared with eye activity, muscle activity or the electrical reference. These recordings cannot apportion it among those sources. A close match between two simultaneous traces also need not predict the next arm reading. On five fourth runs, the cue remained a strong benchmark and the trial’s own scalp record added little by either forecast test. An online controller, calibrated joint angles and transfer beyond this supported right-arm task remain untested.
