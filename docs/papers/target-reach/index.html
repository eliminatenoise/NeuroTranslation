Does knowing the target improve a reach forecast?

Put a cylinder in the middle of a table and mark nine places to put it down. Knowing which place someone has been given tells you something about the journey. How much does it tell you about where the cylinder will be in another four tenths of a second?

Download PDFEditable sourceTechnical methods

In the Ruhr experiment, a person moved the cylinder with one hand. A screen showed the destination. Cameras followed reflective markers on the cylinder. We gave a forecast model the assigned destination, the hand in use and five earlier positions of the object. We then measured the distance between its forecast and the recorded position. Seven people supplied the test recordings. Their movements had played no part in fitting the model.

Between 200 and 600 ms on the acquisition clock the object shifted just 0.117 mm on average. The full model missed by 0.118 mm, and movement alone by 0.114 mm. Once the object had travelled at least 15 mm, the model using both movement and target missed by 12.1 mm. Exchanging the target for a wrong one increased that error to 17.8 mm. Yet a separately trained model given the motion alone missed by 12.2 mm. The observed movement carried most of what was needed for this forecast.

Reading the experiment

When does the clock start?

Imagine starting a stopwatch as soon as the cameras begin recording. The cylinder could still be sitting on the table when the forecast is due. A second stopwatch starts only once the cylinder has travelled a measurable distance. At the same reading on these two watches you can be looking at quite different parts of the reach.

For the primary test, time zero was the first usable optical sample. For the secondary test, we watched the distance from that starting position. Once it reached 15 millimetres and stayed at least that far away for three consecutive camera frames, we started the second clock on the third frame. At 250 frames a second, the first and third readings span eight milliseconds. The movement may already have begun before the crossing. The recordings establish neither clock’s exact timing against the screen or the sound that told the person to move.

What does the model get to know?

The task provides the assigned target. The camera provides the first 200 milliseconds on the chosen clock. The forecast then runs without seeing the rest of that trial. Swapping the target number while keeping the same past positions tests how much the fitted model relies on that number. Removing the target and fitting another model tests whether the earlier positions alone carry the information. A third comparison is especially demanding in this experiment. Look up the average displacement for other people using this hand and this target. Can the model improve on that simple answer?

After the crossing, the average path for the assigned hand and target missed the measured position by 35.4 mm. Adding the five observed positions to the target reduced the error by about 23.3 mm. Knowing the destination leaves room for considerable variation in how far a person has travelled by a given time.

Technical methods

From optical frames to a displacement forecast

Sziburis and colleagues recorded 31 adults transporting a cylinder to nine destinations spaced on a semicircle of radius 250 mm. The optical system and an inertial sensor measured the object. We used the unrotated optical positions. A marker centroid approximates translation of the held object. A single visible marker can also move when the object rotates. Neither measurement locates the wrist joint. The source experiment and apparatus appear in Scientific Data (2025), figures 2–6.

Sampling and exclusions

We selected the first two recorded assignments of each target for each hand before checking measurement quality. We held out every fourth person in the source’s age-ordered numbering, leaving 24 to fit the models and seven to test them. We kept all trials by a person together. The publisher’s exclusions used whole-trial quality information. Our test describes recordings that passed that screen. This is a fixed split of one laboratory dataset. Training and test participants therefore share the apparatus and task.

Of 864 selected development trials, 40 carried publisher optical exclusions and 824 supported all endpoints on both clocks. Of 252 selected test trials, 13 carried the same type of exclusion. All remaining 239 had a detected crossing and all supplied acquisition-clock endpoints (114 left, 125 right). After the crossing one left-hand trial lacked a complete input history. One further left-hand trial lacked all three endpoints, leaving 237 (112 left, 125 right) at each horizon. We made no substitutions. The seven people contributed to every reported hand and horizon.

We chose the visible marker set at the first frame with at least one complete marker. Where both were visible we took their arithmetic mean. Subsequent samples required the same set. Missing measurements and frame-number gaps break a string of qualifying crossing frames. The source’s camera processing can include filled gaps without marking them per sample. Submillimetre shifts can also include optical measurement noise.

Nominal input times were 0, 50, 100, 150 and 200 ms. We used the most recent valid native sample at or before each time, within one 4 ms period. At 400, 600 and 800 ms we used the first valid sample at or after the named time within one period. For each endpoint we subtracted the final input position, producing three displacements in metres. Unavailable endpoints contributed no training loss or test error. Neither future trial duration nor future positions served as inputs.

What the models learned

Each of the five time steps carried the position relative to the first observation, the change since the previous observation, the sample time, a hand indicator and an indicator for the target and hand together (27 inputs). One attention block compared the five time steps. Two attention heads shared 16 hidden features. A 32-unit feedforward layer and the last time step’s state supplied nine outputs, three axes for each of three horizons.

Technical methods and interpretation

What the comparisons can support

The full, motion-only and target-only Transformers shared initial weights and trial order within each of three seeds (17, 41 and 73). Motion-only omitted target identity. Target-only omitted all six motion components. All retained hand and time. We fitted each clock separately for 40 epochs in batches of 64, using AdamW, learning rate 0.0015, weight decay 0.001, gradient norm limit 1 and no dropout. Training data supplied continuous input centring and scaling and a common root-mean-square output scale. Squared errors gave each available person equal weight at each horizon and each horizon equal weight.

The linear comparisons used the same flattened inputs and a ridge penalty of 0.01. The stationary control forecast zero displacement. Constant velocity projected the last observed velocity to the nominal endpoint. The target-and-hand mean averaged each training person’s displacement and then averaged across those people. The wrong-target control ran the full model eight more times per trial, once for each alternative target. We averaged those eight distances to the true measurement. No participant was asked to execute a different movement for this test.

For each seed and trial we computed Euclidean error, the straight-line distance between the two points in three dimensions. We averaged seed errors, then trials within a person, then people. For contrasts we resampled people with replacement 10,000 times (seed 20261122) and took the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of the paired differences. These intervals condition on the fitted models and these seven people. Analyses by hand, additional horizons and the displacement-crossing clock are secondary. No multiplicity adjustment was applied.

A different kind of instruction

In NEMO-Lowlands, the prompt was a word and picture naming an object. People improvised how to depict it. In our separate Lowlands test, 100 first attempts across 22 archive groups covered 33 concepts. Using early pose and concept, the model missed subsequent wrist displacement relative to the same-side shoulder by 55.80 mm, averaged across wrists and groups. Motion alone missed by 56.01 mm. Their paired difference was −0.22 mm (95% interval −3.79 to +3.49). Swapping the concept gave a difference of +0.38 mm (−1.45 to +2.27, full minus swapped). Staying still did better at 41.35 mm. The recording-relative endpoint followed the last input by a median 0.842 s. Neither gesture comparison established a benefit from supplying the object label. The archive grouping cannot verify independent people, and the combined prompt cannot isolate an effect of words. Neither experiment tested sentences.
