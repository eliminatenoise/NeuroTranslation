One stride, two force plates

A bare foot can carry a walker’s weight and leave no trace on either force plate. It only has to land a little farther along the floor. Where the feet went proved a better guide to the next reading than the electrical activity of the leg muscles alone. On eight previously unseen walkers, adding the electrodes to a model that already tracked the feet and pelvis raised its average error by 5.01 newtons.

Schreiber and Moissenet recorded adults walking across a ten-metre walkway. We followed one stride from right footfall to right footfall, aligning 89 slow strides from 30 walkers. Their right foot left the ground at 68.1% of the stride on average (between-person standard deviation 2.3 percentage points). A full stride took 1.70 seconds on average (SD 0.17 s).

At mid-stride the right heel marker was still just 4.3 cm high. By 68% it had risen to 19.1 cm and the shin signal had climbed from 12.75 to 73.88 µV. The right-contact force curve ends at 58%, well before mean foot-off. Only four strides from three walkers met the checks for a clean, side-specific contact, and only 2–58% of the cycle had the required three contributors. The blank portion marks missing support. Measurement and study design

Which foot made that reading?

Two plates sat flush with the floor. Walkers crossed them in both directions. Plate 1 could catch a left foot on one pass and a right foot on another. Before each session the operators calibrated the cameras, defined the laboratory axes and zeroed the plates. Reconstruction residuals from the camera calibration were below 2 mm. That figure measures how closely the camera rays agreed on a point. We checked the plate corners, channel map and force sign. On these flat plates the stored local vertical axes point down, so an upward reaction equals the negative of the local vertical force in newtons.

Schreiber and Moissenet’s Supplementary Table 2 identifies the plate assigned to each foot in each trial. Its two digits give the right then the left assignment. Codes 12 and 21 indicate reliable contacts on distinct plates. A zero denotes an unavailable or unreliable assignment. Even a reliable assignment tells us only that the foot made a suitable contact somewhere during the trial.

What the instruments picked up

Cameras tracked 52 skin markers 100 times per second. In the stride reference, individual marker curves have 29 or 30 contributors per phase. Electrical recording at the skin, called surface electromyography (EMG), sampled eight right-leg muscles at 1,500 Hz, on the same nominal clock as the plates. To show voltage amplitude in microvolts (µV) in Figure 1 we took the square root of the mean squared voltage in a centred 50 ms window. At mid-stride the shin averaged 12.75 µV, with a between-person SD of 17.26 µV. The voltage depends on the tissue and electrode placement. Measuring muscle tension or a signal travelling along a nerve would require other instruments.

For Figure 1 a right-foot plate assignment had to agree with a complete load bout near the recorded strike and foot-off. In a view from above, four right-foot markers had to fall over the plate near mid-contact and the left-foot marker centroid had to lie outside. The ordinary plate curves required reliable plate data somewhere in the trial. They retained 75 trials for plate 1 and 70 for plate 2.

The article gives a 5 N cutoff. The authors’ force-processing code sets it to 50 N and replaces missing samples with zeros before filtering. Thus a released zero can reflect a foot landing elsewhere, a small force removed by processing or missing instrument data. Neither experiment measures load borne on ordinary floor.

Both experiments use the three lowest-numbered available repeats of the barefoot, metronome-paced C2 condition (target 0.4–0.8 m/s). The first 30 of the 50 people in original identifier order were assigned to fitting, the next ten to assessment, and the final ten held in reserve. Each experiment had its own eligibility rules. No reserve recordings entered either fit or score.

Held-person assessment · Combined plate load

Can the electrodes improve the forecast?

Give a model the last 240 ms of movement. From each of eight 30 ms slices it gets the positions of six markers, one on each heel, forefoot and side of the pelvis. A second version also gets the electrical amplitudes of eight right-leg muscles. Each Transformer learns which earlier slices to weight when predicting upward force. Both predict the average sum over a 10 ms block, centred 100 ms after the final 10 ms block of input on the recorded clock.

Only recordings with two reliably assigned plates entered this comparison. Twenty-five training trials had incomplete contact assignments and three lacked an exact table match. Thirteen assessment trials had incomplete assignments. That left 25 training walkers (62 trials, 4,846 windows) and eight assessment walkers (17 trials, 1,297 windows). All eligible times within those trials counted, including recorded zeros. Each trial counted equally within a walker, each walker equally in the final score. Fitting fixed the model weights before assessment.

Lower is better. Mean absolute error gives each newton of error equal weight. Root mean square error weighs large misses more heavily. Ridge is a regression with a penalty on large fitted weights. The nonlinear marker model also compared each history with examples from training. Carrying the earlier load forward requires an extra measurement from the plates.

The marker Transformer missed by 65.19 N on average. Adding muscle voltage raised the error to 70.20 N. The paired change was +5.01 N (95% interval −0.04 to +10.23 N, from 10,000 resamples of whole walkers). The combined model did beat nonlinear marker ridge by 21.06 N (paired interval −34.75 to −7.24 N for combined minus ridge).

We also replaced the electrical input with a roughly pose-matched snippet from another repeat by the same walker. Six people had comparable repeats (15 trials, 1,143 windows). Aligned voltage gave 72.20 N against 73.28 N for the replacement, a difference of −1.08 N (−7.31 to +4.67 N). The prespecified test required at least eight people, at least 5% lower error and an interval wholly below zero against all three comparisons. Only the nonlinear ridge comparison passed. These recordings give no evidence meeting that test for added electrical information.

Held-person assessment · Plate assigned to the right foot

A reading under the right foot

At the first recorded right strike, the heel marker and the marker at the base of the second toe had to lie on or inside the assigned plate. One of 17 otherwise eligible assessment trials failed this check. Every main model knew the time since strike. At 140, 240, 340 and 440 ms it used four 60 ms slices of history to predict that plate’s reading 100 ms later.

Training used 203 times in 61 walks from 25 people. All 53 target readings from eight other walkers (16 trials) were positive, spanning 422.75–924.84 N. Performance on an unloaded plate remains untested. The shared NeuroTranslate model’s encoder converts the marker sequence into a learned set of numbers. We kept it fixed and fitted a regression to predict load from those numbers and the original inputs.

The shared combined model overestimated load by 34.51 N on average. Adding EMG to the shared marker model reduced mean absolute error by 8.75 N. Five of eight walkers improved, and the paired 95% interval for combined minus marker error ran from −28.99 to +9.03 N. The combined model beat the simple combined ridge in only two of eight walkers. It beat the elapsed-time table and the scrambled encoder in four of eight each.

For six people with another suitable repeat (14 walks, 44 times), aligned EMG gave 83.26 N against 85.02 N after substituting that person’s EMG at the same elapsed time in another walk. Five of six person-level error differences were within 10−10 N of zero. Neither the prespecified test for added EMG information nor the test for a benefit from the trained encoder passed.

What “100 ms later” means here

The authors filled marker gaps, removed the full-trial mean from each muscle signal, and filtered markers (6 Hz), force (15 Hz) and muscle voltage (30–300 Hz) forward and backward. They cropped the recordings around annotated events. A stored input can therefore reflect later raw samples. In both forecasts the centres of the last 10 ms frame block and target block are 100 ms apart. The nearest analog samples are about 91 ms apart. Sensor-specific delays remain unverified. A live prediction or a physiological delay would need a fresh test on signals processed as they arrive.
