What helps predict the next movement?

If you have already watched half a second of a stride, does the word walk or run tell you anything more? In a separate experiment, if you know where a rat has been moving, does a second of brain-cell firing help you predict how far it will go?

A word and eleven poses

We used optical recordings from the Carnegie Mellon University Motion Capture Database. The model follows 18 sites on the head, trunk, arms and legs. Some sites are averages of camera-tracked skin markers. Each forecast starts with eleven poses from the preceding half second. A Transformer, a model that learns relationships among those observations, predicts the next twelve poses at 50-millisecond intervals. A second Transformer sees the same movement history with the action label removed. The two models used the same architecture and training procedure.

Thirty-one training recordings supplied the examples. Before fitting, we set aside twelve additional recordings from six unused catalogue groups. All twelve passed the native 120 Hz recording checks. Taking every sixth camera frame gave a common 20 Hz clock without interpolation. The forward direction comes from the final observed waist orientation. A later frame cannot set the orientation or tell the model where a step begins.

Two brain regions and the next quarter-second

A camera followed lights on a rat’s head as it moved around a W-shaped track. We asked how far the tracked point would travel in a straight line from its last observed position to its position around 250 milliseconds later. Distance alone leaves its direction unknown.

Recordings by Justin Shin and Shantanu Jadhav supplied synchronized timestamps for camera positions and spikes from recorded units in hippocampal CA1 and prefrontal cortex. We counted spikes in four consecutive quarter-second bins, separately for each region. Counts were divided by bin duration and the number of recorded units in that region. Pooling by region lets the calculation use recordings from another rat with a different set of units.

We fitted two regularized linear models on 824 instants from three rats. One saw the recent movement. The other also saw regional firing. The test used 549 eligible instants across 45 trips from three other rats. To check whether timing mattered, we ran the combined model again using firing from a completed earlier trip of the same rat with the camera history held fixed.

Trips counted equally within each animal, and each animal counted once in the reported mean. Correctly timed firing lowered the mean from 2.898 to 2.870 cm. The prespecified criterion required improvement over both controls in every rat. It failed. Even on KL8, continuing at the last observed speed gave the lowest error. Averaging all instants together gives KL8 nearly half the weight because it supplied 267 of the 549 forecasts.

The two experiments measure different things. The stride task forecasts where body markers move relative to the pelvis and where the pelvis moves in a room. The track task forecasts one distance from headstage tracking. Neither experiment measures a muscle contraction, a person’s intended action or a closed sensory feedback loop.
