A week later, does seeing the arm improve a forecast of the brain?

A macaque reaches for a target. Give a model one more tenth of a second to watch the arm. Can it make a better guess about the electrical activity that comes next?

Chowdhury, Glaser and Miller recorded the animal reaching with a robot handle on September 7 and 14, 2017. We fitted the models on the first day and tested them on the second. The camera followed the hand and elbow, the robot tracked the handle, and electrodes in a region of sensory cortex called area 2 recorded brief electrical events, or spikes.

The answer depended on where the animal reached

Across 37 electrode contacts, seeing how the arm moved reduced count-prediction error in workspace 1. In workspace 2 it increased the error. Substituting movement from a different reach made the forecast worse in both workspaces. The correct reach carried information, yet using it could still hurt a forecast trained seven days earlier.

We also asked whether past spikes could sharpen a forecast of the arm itself. They did not reliably improve the hand and elbow together. For 39 muscle–tendon paths calculated from the tracked arm, predicting that each length would stay put gave the smallest average error in both workspaces. These comparisons concern one animal on one held-out day.

Which reaches count?

September 7 supplied 519 short windows from 198 rewarded reach sequences, all in workspace 1. A sequence can contain several go signals. We fixed the date rule before inspecting test measurements and selected the first later recording of the same animal doing the same two-workspace task. The date was September 14. All fitted weights, scaling and comparison rules were fixed before access to its numerical signals.

What the model could know, and when

  1. Before the cutoff The last 200 ms of arm positions, handle positions, cortical counts and past go cues are available. The cutoff, k, lies five 10-ms samples after the selected go signal. Only the second or third go qualified on September 14.
  2. Next 100 ms At k, forecast ten arm positions through k+9. Once that interval has passed, the measured path can update a forecast of subsequent spikes. One separate comparison also sees this interval’s spike counts.
  3. Following 100 ms Count the spikes on each contact from k+10 through k+19, after the movement used for the update. All count forecasts refer to this same outcome.

Eligibility required a rewarded sequence, sufficient history and future, a first target-start stream matching the first go, and time before another go. Workspace, reward, eventual end and later cues selected the windows. They were withheld as predictor inputs. Target locations were unavailable. All 493 candidates passed the numerical checks. Eight lacked an earlier reach from the same block, workspace and go ordinal for the borrowed-input control.

Which forecast benefits from seeing the arm?

Imagine pausing the recording just after a go cue. From the last five samples, extend the arm’s recent velocity forward by 100 ms. Resume the recording. For each of the eight position coordinates, subtract that extrapolated displacement from the displacement that actually occurred. This difference tells the model how the reach departed from its recent course.

Our earlier count forecast uses the previous position, velocity, 200 ms of spike totals and past cue timing. A second fitted model also receives the eight movement differences. Both predict the next count on each electrode. The borrowed-movement control gives the second model those differences from the latest eligible different reach at the same go ordinal, in the same block and workspace. Recipient spike history stays fixed. No later reach can supply a missing donor.

What counts as a spike on the same contact?

An electrode records electrical events from nearby tissue. The experimenters grouped events with similar waveforms and gave each group a local number, called a sort. Zero denotes events left unassigned. A numbered group can disappear or change between days. We combined counts at the same acquisition contact and treated the numbers as local labels.

The September 7 file lists 74 columns on 52 contacts. Each September 14 block lists 98 columns on 60 contacts. Of 38 contacts represented throughout, 37 have a sort-zero column throughout and qualify for the primary total. That total includes each exported column on each eligible contact once (57 columns at training, 60 per test block). Contact 67 lacks a sort-zero column on September 7. We leave it out of that total.

Identical numbers can conceal a different population

The 17 shared positive contacts account for 18 locally sorted columns on each day. Contact 15 went from two positive groups to one, and contact 53 from one to two. The other 15 kept the same number of positive groups. Five training contacts and 16 test contacts lacked positive coverage on the opposite day. We counted observed zeros and excluded missing coverage. These records do not identify which neurons, recording gains or detection thresholds stayed the same.

What if the model has heard the latest spikes?

The earlier baseline knew the spikes only up to the cutoff. A second experiment gives both competitors the same additional 100 ms of spikes before they predict the next 100 ms. One keeps the earlier arm position. The other also sees where the arm was halfway through and at the end of the intervening movement. Each predicts positive-sort totals on the same 17 shared contacts.

How the two count fits differ

The main update uses a separate regression for each contact. Its exponential output predicts a positive expected count. We fit training-scaled inputs with a squared-coefficient penalty of 0.01, leaving the intercept free. For a coordinate p, the new input is [p(k+9) − p(k−1)] − 10[p(k−1) − p(k−5)]/4. We scale each coordinate before combining metres and centimetres.

The separate late-spike fit uses a 0.05 penalty. It pools positive sorts on each contact, groups spikes into 50-ms totals, takes log(1 + total) and scales with September 7 values. All four fitted variants use the last known arm position, its recent change and past cues. The measured variant gets two extra displacements. The borrowed variant changes only those displacements. The fixed fit also permits zeroing the displacements while keeping the recipient’s recent spikes.

Can past spikes improve a forecast of the arm?

Turn the question around. Pause at the original cutoff and give a model the preceding 200 ms of arm motion and go cues. It predicts the next ten positions. A small correction model can then use the same history plus old counts on the 37 shared contacts. During this prediction the next 100 ms of movement and spikes remain hidden.

The correction model learned how far to adjust a frozen forecast, with both versions given the same number of trainable weights. Its borrowed-count control replaced only the 200 ms of cortical history, keeping the recipient’s own position and cue. The hand and elbow errors are three-dimensional distances in millimetres. How far does a muscle’s path change?

Trace the route of a tendon and its muscle across a moving joint. As the joint angle changes, the distance along that route can change. The original experiment fitted a macaque arm model in OpenSim to optical markers and computed 39 muscle–tendon path lengths. Here we forecast the change in each modeled length relative to the last known sample.

A fitted map converts the eight movement coordinates into length changes. Its coefficients depend on the starting pose. Holding all arm coordinates still gives zero change. A second, direct regression forecasts length changes from earlier pose and cue history. Both fits used September 7 only. Feeding the path map the intervening observed arm trajectory gives it information available 100 ms later, so that row is a retrospective diagnostic.

What the comparison leaves open

A simple forecast can be hard to beat over 100 ms when true changes are small. That makes the no-change error an important reference. It cannot tell us how much force the tissue produced. An independent recording of the biological quantity of interest would be needed to test that claim. A cleaner test of generalization would keep an additional day and animal untouched while fixing the same channel, clock and workspace rules.

Methods, recordings and readers

What an error bar covers

For a count y and forecast mean m, Poisson deviance is 2[y log(y/m) − y + m]. The log term contributes zero at y = 0. Expected totals have a scoring floor of 0.000001. We weight contacts equally within each window, then give each supported sequence equal weight. Lower values mean closer forecasts. Differences are computed on the very same sequences, so their intervals can be narrower than either absolute interval.

Within each workspace, we drew overlapping runs of eight consecutive sequences with replacement, independently within each recording block. Runs could not wrap around or cross the recording break. We retained unsupported sequence slots, truncated to each block’s assigned roster length, pooled supported sequences and repeated 5,000 times (seed 20261125). Brackets give the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles. An additional check sampled individual sequences within each block. For the primary update minus earlier forecast it gave [−0.326, −0.040] and [+0.041, +0.168] in workspaces 1 and 2. None of these intervals samples a new animal or recording day.

The optics supplied shoulder-relative hand and near-elbow marker positions in metres. The encoder supplied handle position in centimetres in its own frame. Reconstruction, interpolation, bidirectional filtering and offline spike sorting constrain the timing. The comparison runs in software on the saved 10-ms grid. It cannot establish live latency, a causal sensory route or transfer to a human limb.
