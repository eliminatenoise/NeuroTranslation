The next knee angle and the rhythm of a walk

Watch a few seconds of someone walking at a steady pace and you may already have seen the next bend of their knee. In one treadmill study, replaying an earlier stride beat a model that saw only 0.4 seconds of movement. Give a model four seconds and train it to correct the replay, and the result changes. The same recordings let us ask a different question. Can electrical signals from the legs tell us how often the knees bend?

Fifteen frames ahead

Katmah and colleagues recorded healthy young adults walking on a treadmill. At one belt speed, 0.5 metres per second, body-worn inertial sensors supplied estimates of how far each joint bent. We took five snapshots of both hips, knees and ankles across 0.4 seconds. A small Transformer, a model trained to combine information across those snapshots, estimated both knee angles 15 stored frames after the last snapshot. At 60 frames per second, that is a quarter of a second in the file.

What if the model can look back a whole stride?

A rule with four seconds of knee history searched for an earlier stride whose movement resembled the latest second. It copied the angles 15 frames beyond that earlier match. The rule needed no training. It needed more history, leaving 40,201 forecast points on which to compare the two methods directly.

The earlier stride won for 10 of 12 people. On those same points its mean error was 3.535°, compared with 4.465° for the Transformer. The average advantage was 0.93° (95% interval 0.36° to 1.57°, resampling whole people and preserving each person’s paired errors).

Matched walking histories

Give each forecast the same four seconds

The previous stride gives a starting answer for each knee. Can a model learn how far to move that answer? This time every method sees the same 240 stored frames through the same cutoff. Each must predict the same two angles 15 frames later.

Learning a correction on 39 walkers

We first matched the final second of knee movement to an earlier part of the walk. One method simply replayed the match. Two others learned a correction from past angles and features of the matching stride. One used a weighted sum of those features. The other used a small Transformer trained for this task, which learns how much information to take from different past moments.

The task-trained model came closest for all 12 walkers. Its mean advantage over replay was 0.856° (95% interval 0.628° to 1.089°, resampling people). Against the linear correction it gained 0.731° (0.579° to 0.891°). These people had been kept out of training, but we knew their earlier forecast results when designing this comparison.

Did the earlier movement model carry anything over?

NeuroTranslate already had a component trained to recognise patterns in wrist and rat movement. We kept that component fixed and gave it four one-second summaries of the knees. A new calculation used the patterns, the summaries and the stride match to correct the forecast. It missed by 3.465° on average. The summaries and stride match alone gave 3.460°. We then scrambled the earlier component’s learned numbers once and fitted the correction again. It gave 3.422°. The success of the task-trained model therefore gives us no evidence here that earlier training on the other movements helped.

Trial-level rhythm

How often does the knee bend?

Slide a recording of repeated knee bends one second along a timeline. Every bend moves, and the number of bends per minute stays the same. That distinction lets us compare the rhythm recorded by two instruments even when we cannot line up each movement between them.

One number from each instrument

Skin electrodes on the legs measured electrical activity near the muscles. The surface electromyography (EMG) files contain a summary for each of 12 channels, the average size of the voltage regardless of its sign. The movement files contain a bend angle for each knee. A person and a treadmill speed identify the corresponding pair of files.

In each file we used seconds 5 to 45 on its own clock. We asked how strongly each signal repeated at rates from 27 to 90 cycles per minute. For each channel we removed its average level, gave the middle of the interval more weight (a Hann taper), and calculated the power at each possible rate. We scaled those powers to add up to one so that louder channels had no extra say. Averaging across the 12 muscle channels gave one strongest rate. Averaging across the two knees gave another. Each is a repeating cycle rate. Neither instrument counted footfalls in this test.

How small is 0.027 cycles per minute?

Nine of the 11 pairs selected the same point on the analysis grid. The other two differed by one point, 0.15 cycles per minute. Averaging the two differences and nine zeros gives 0.027. The matched estimate beat the training median for ten people and tied for one. It beat the wrong-person comparison for all eleven.

One extra cycle in a 40-second window corresponds to 1.5 cycles per minute (60 ÷ 40), the characteristic frequency scale of the analysis. Searching it in 0.15-cycle increments gives a finer grid on which to compare peaks. The three decimal places describe agreement between estimated peaks.

Acquisition and interpretation

When did a muscle signal occur relative to a bend?

A claim about the order of two events needs a time reference that survives the trip from instrument to file. The study authors describe a shared trigger during acquisition. We inspected the published exports for one development walk, S1 at 0.5 m/s, to see which timing evidence a reader can recover.

Scalp electrodes
The 300 Hz scalp electrical recording (EEG) contains 110 trigger changes. Its header reports a 40 ms firmware filter delay. Neither gait test used scalp signals.
Muscle electrodes
The 12 mean absolute voltage channels carry time labels about 16.05 times per second. The export has no matching trigger column. The labels alone leave open which original samples contributed to each average.
Movement sensors
The inertial measurement unit (IMU) export includes numbered frames at 60 Hz. Its marker table has no event rows with which to match the other devices.

The recorded angles introduce a second timing question. The headers for six of the 51 angle files carried the Xsens “HD” label. This processing uses the whole recording and can refine an earlier angle using later information. The other 45 said “Live”. Neither label measures the delay from a person bending a knee to that angle becoming available to a predictor. Xsens manual, §9.1

What would settle the timing?

Match identifiable events across the devices, measure clock drift from start to finish, and establish when each muscle average and joint estimate became available. A live forecast also needs a separate measurement of the physical knee against which to test its error. Here the target came from the same reconstruction as the inputs. Agreement on rhythm leaves contraction onset, neural intent and the direction of influence unresolved. Other speeds, patients and overground walking remain untested.
