Will the arm forecast work for someone new?

A picture asks a seated person to bend the right elbow. The instruction is the same from one person to the next. The starting position, the speed of the movement and the readings on the glove can all change. How much can a model carry from one arm to another?

We used Patrick Ofner and colleagues’ supported right-arm recordings. The support measured elbow bending and forearm rotation. A glove measured the base of the index finger. Six movement cues and one rest cue supplied the instructions. We fitted the models on the first seven people and tested the frozen weights on five others. Three further people stayed in reserve. No imagined actions entered either calculation.

What counts as a forecast?

A quarter second after the cue, the model receives four short intervals of mechanical history. Each contains three instrument levels and three within-interval changes. It forecasts how far each reading will move in the next 0.5, 1 or 1.5 seconds. The outcome is an observed change in that instrument’s stored units. The file does not supply a verified conversion to degrees.

Fitting the shared model on seven people cut its error by 36% compared with the same model fitted on one. Still, the group’s typical movement for each cue gave a closer average forecast. Once a person had completed one run, their own typical movement for the cue was closer still on the later trials.

What does learning the time sequence buy?

Four tokens represent the four intervals. A fifth represents the task and the latest sensor levels. A small Transformer learns which tokens to combine. Its input projection, task and position embeddings, attention weights and output weights all change during training. The comparison network receives the same information and uses ordinary dense layers. A linear fit, the cue alone and no change give the model progressively simpler alternatives to beat.

On 630 trials from five people, the task-trained Transformer averaged 0.493. With no cue it scored 0.552; giving the same model the wrong cue raised the error to 0.667. Every assessment participant benefited from the correct cue in both comparisons. The cue alone scored 0.467. The Transformer beat that simpler model for only one person.

The Transformer beat the dense network for all five people. Against the linear cue-and-motion fit, its average advantage was smaller than the specified 5%, and its paired interval included zero. The tests for an advantage from attention and for a net improvement over the simple forecasts both failed.

How much is one earlier run worth?

A second study kept the time encoder from NeuroTranslate and fitted new outputs on the seven people. The same test measured the earlier model fitted on one person. To see what the person’s own past measurements could add, we tried a correction. For each cue, we took the median of observed change minus forecast change in run one. Six examples of a cue supplied half that difference as a correction. Runs two and three supplied the test.

On the 420 later trials, the correction lowered error from 0.521 to 0.484. All five people improved relative to the uncorrected model, with a paired 95% interval for the difference of −0.052 to −0.023. The person’s own cue median scored 0.429. A first-run correction helped the encoder but missed the requirement to beat all three comparison methods.

Adding a constant to every reading from a sensor leaves its measured change unchanged. Two additional fits left the absolute starting levels out of the input. This check was specified after inspecting fitting participants’ starting levels and before opening the five assessment people. Removing the three starting levels reduced linear and encoder error by 2.15% and 1.84%. Both improvements fell below the prespecified 5%.

Did the sensors change in the same direction?

The scalp cap and the arm instruments recorded the same cued trials. In a separate descriptive analysis, we asked how many of the seven fitting participants had a higher, lower or unchanged reading after each cue. We first compared each participant with their own pre-cue baseline, then counted the signs across people. This preserves a distinction between genuine zero change in a working channel and an unavailable measurement.

What does a scaled error hide?

At 1.5 seconds ahead, the task-trained Transformer missed the elbow, forearm and index readings by 2.564, 2.886 and 0.0398 on their respective native scales. For the group cue median the corresponding errors were 2.052, 2.226 and 0.0417. One method can have lower overall error while another is closer on a particular sensor.

Five assessment participants give limited information about how results would vary across a larger population. The clocks are nominally 512 Hz for events and scalp signals and 64 Hz for the mechanical sensors. Device latency, individual physical calibration and performance in a live setting remain unverified.
