Does a muscle bundle’s angle help predict its next change in length?

NeuroTranslation · Experimental research supplement · 21 November 2026

Lift the front of your foot. A muscle beside your shinbone pulls on a tendon to help raise it. An ultrasound probe can watch a bundle of fibres in that muscle change length and tilt. If we already know how long the bundle has been, what does its tilt tell us about what comes next?

We trained two forecasting models on four volunteers from Zhang and colleagues’ AnkleImage study. Each forecast used the recent history of a bundle in an ultrasound image and predicted its change in length ten tracking steps later. We compared forecasts with the matching angle, with length alone and with the angle from a different trial by the same person. On four new people, both models made a smaller average error with the matching angle than with either alternative. Neither met its predetermined test for an angle benefit. A simple linear forecast using recent lengths came closer than the angle-aware transformer in each family.

What the probe could see

Zhang and colleagues asked a seated volunteer to lift and lower the forefoot in four cycles with the foot suspended. The probe looked into the tibialis anterior, the muscle along the front of the shin. A line fitted to a visible bundle of muscle fibres, called a fascicle, supplied an estimate of its length in the image plane. The authors also reported its tilt, the pennation angle, conventionally measured relative to a sheet of connective tissue. The separate orientation of that sheet can only be checked in some exports.

More dots can mean the same evidence

In the first development recording, the 9,910 display values could be reconstructed from 992 tracking rows by drawing straight lines between successive estimates. We kept the original rows, called knots. The opposite shortcut would also fail. One volunteer (A02) really did have 9,920 knots in each trial. The other three development volunteers had 992. Ten steps in each file therefore names a place in a sequence. It does not specify a common elapsed time.

We checked the released length and angle against the authors’ units, field labels and numerical exports in each trial. The angle came from a difference of two saved orientations in A01 and a sum in A02 and A04. All eight test recordings reproduced the reported angle by a sum. For A03, the released curves followed the first numbered fascicle. Its sheet orientation could not be recovered independently. Treating the next numbered fascicle as that sheet would change the biological meaning of the angle.

Hide ten steps. Predict the change.

At each eligible cutoff i, both models saw 21 original lengths ending at that row. The target was the tracked length at i + 10 minus the length at i, in millimetres. A negative change means shortening. Cutoffs began at row index 20 (counting from zero) and advanced ten steps. All 31 rows from the start of the history to the endpoint had to belong to one continuous, qualified segment. Adjacent forecasts shared history. Each ordinary forecast used measurements through its cutoff. The wrong-trial comparison selected a donor retrospectively by rank within two completed recordings.

Two ways to learn from the past

The specialist learned for this one task. It arranged the 20 intervals into five groups of four. Each group described length and its change, plus the mean and change in the four earlier angles. The last angle preceded the cutoff by one row. A small transformer learned how much weight to give different parts of this history, then predicted the signed change. The length-only twin used the same training recipe with both angle features withheld.

The shared model reused a movement encoder from NeuroTranslate 0.0. Its weights stayed fixed. Four groups of five intervals described length, change, mean deviation from the straight-line trend, angle, angle change and change in the longitudinal span. The last angle came from the cutoff itself. A fitted linear readout corrected the previous ten-step change using these measurements and the encoder’s output. We also fitted a readout with the measurements alone and one with a fixed, randomly initialized encoder.

Both families saw trials 1 and 2 from A01–A04. For the development results, we trained on three people and predicted the fourth in turn. We then fitted on all four and froze the models, scoring methods and source selection before opening any new participant. A09 remained in reserve.

Two geometric guesses

The specialist extended the last ten-step change in longitudinal span (x), ending at the last angle it could see. It divided by the cosine of that last angle to convert the span change into a length change. This assumes a fixed local direction for the conversion. The shared model used the cutoff angle, extended the last ten-step change in x, and held the transverse span (h) fixed. It found the new length as the hypotenuse of the resulting triangle. These assumptions can yield different answers from the very same history.

How the forecasts compared

For each forecast, we took the absolute difference between predicted and observed change. We averaged errors within a trial, averaged the two trials for each person and then averaged people. One development volunteer supplied 1,978 of the 2,560 eligible cutoffs but only one quarter of the weight. All scales and fitted parameters came from the training people for that stage.

On development folds, the length-only linear models had the lowest mean error in both families. Adding angle raised the specialist’s error in all four people, yet swapping in the angle from their other trial raised the average error still further. The same order held for the shared model’s averages. Neither development study supported an angle benefit. The pretrained shared angle model also lost to the linear angle model and the random encoder in all four folds.

All eight new recordings qualified. Each supplied 992 original knots and 97 forecasts, for 776 forecasts per family. In the specialist, adding angle reduced error from 0.2365 to 0.2330 mm, while a linear forecast from lengths alone reached 0.2226 mm. In the shared model the corresponding errors were 0.2258, 0.2192 and 0.2167 mm. A linear forecast with both inputs had the lowest shared-family error (0.2152 mm). The pretrained full model beat the random encoder by 0.0023 mm on average, with two of four people improving.

What does the angle add?

Swapping the angle asks whether a model uses the pairing it received. We kept the weights, target and length history fixed and took angles from a corresponding point in the same person’s other trial. The specialist used the nearest proportional cutoff rank. The shared model rounded down its proportional rank and recalculated the longitudinal spans. Swapping can disrupt shared tracking errors as well as movement patterns.

The specialist needed a lower mean error and gains in at least three people against every specified control. Matching angles beat swapped angles in all four and length alone in three. Length-only linear regression still beat the full specialist on average. For the length-only specialist itself, all four improved against zero and typical change, but only two improved against repeating the recent change and two against linear regression. Both prespecified pairs of length-only tests fell short.

For the specialist, the person-level change from adding angle ranged from an extra 0.0063 mm of error to a reduction of 0.0188 mm. The shared model needed at least 5% lower mean error than both its length-only and wrong-angle controls, with gains against both in at least three people. Its reductions were 2.94% and 9.20%, respectively. One person improved against both. Even agreement in all four people would yield a two-sided sign-test probability of 0.125 under equally likely positive and negative changes. The thousands of cutoffs overlap and come from repeated ankle lifts. Wider claims about people or tasks would require additional independent participants, reliable source clocks and independent measurements of the tissue motion.

Offline tracking may include smoothing or manual correction. The study reported synchronized acquisition, but the timing correspondence between individual exported image estimates, ankle measurements and electrical signals was unavailable here. These fits cannot establish a live response delay, muscle force, neural drive, clinical benefit or a unique three-dimensional trajectory.
