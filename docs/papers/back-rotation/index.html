How far will a sensor on your back turn in a tenth of a second?

@eliminatenoise · Experimental study · 21 November 2026

Suppose you know how far a sensor just turned and whether its wearer was asked to walk or stand still. Would voltages from two patches on their back help you forecast the next turn?

Gracia and colleagues recorded both measurements in the same walking experiment. We fitted two forecasts on four volunteers and set aside four other volunteers from the same study. Six test trials from three volunteers identify all four channels needed to measure an angle. Two trials from the fourth volunteer do not. We report forecasts for the three with verified channels and keep the planned four-person test open. Original experiment [1]

Repeating the recent turn missed by 0.445° on average. With the task and motion history, the specialist missed by 0.361° and the shared model by 0.376°. Adding both voltage patches increased the errors to 0.382° and 0.385° on these same three people.

Measurement and test design2 / 5

Where does a turn begin and end?

In Experiment 2, volunteers alternated five seconds of walking with five seconds of standing, with cues between them. One grid of 32 electrodes lay near the fourth neck vertebra (C4) and another near the second lumbar vertebra (L2). Each grid formed eight rows of four contacts, spaced 10 mm apart. Voltage in microvolts was measured against a reference on the right shoulder blade. A strap held the orientation sensor against the back. Recording protocol and data [2]

A sensor can swivel five degrees and return to where it started. Its net turn would be zero even though it travelled through ten degrees. Our target is that unsigned net turn, between 0° and 180°. It loses direction. For two endpoint orientations recorded as four-number descriptions (quaternions), normalize each to unit length, then compute

θ = 2 × arccos(clip(|q · r|, 0, 1)) × 180/π.

The absolute value handles the two signs that can describe one orientation. Clipping keeps rounding error inside the inverse cosine’s domain. Both forecasts also constrain their output to the physical range.

Who and when counts?

We selected two trials (02 and 03) per person. One development trial had already supplied simple reference scores. We fixed the design before opening further development records, held out one entire person at a time in four development rounds, then fitted all four together. We fixed the models and scoring rules before opening the four intended test volunteers’ records. In one volunteer’s two files, the four labels read quaternion 2, quaternion 3, quaternion 4 and “BufferChannel.” That leaves no verified first component. The file contents could reflect incorrect labels or incorrect channels. We kept the models fixed, excluded both files from the descriptive scores and left the ninth volunteer in reserve.

Code 611 means instructed standing and 621 means instructed walking. Scoring requires both intervals to stay inside the same labelled task block. Its later label determines eligibility only. Preparation, initial calibration and transitions lie outside this test. The model sees only the task known at the cutoff. These codes say what the volunteer was asked to do.

Results · The task-trained transformer3 / 5

Does either patch earn its place?

Start with a forecast anyone can make. If the device just turned one degree, predict one degree again. A second approach predicts the typical angle seen in training for the current task. A third uses a weighted sum of recent movement measurements, with a penalty on large fitted weights (ridge regression).

The specialist transformer cuts the past 100 ms into ten 10 ms pieces. For each it reads the turn during that piece and the turn since the start of the history. Each patch supplies three summaries of how voltage varied across time and contacts. Attention lets the last piece weight earlier pieces before the model adds a correction to the recent turn. Matching copies learn with neither patch, one patch or both. Specialist forecast and methods [3]

Give each forecast the wrong moment

Keep the fitted model, the person, task and trial fixed. Shift both electrical histories together halfway round the list of eligible times for that task, about 24 seconds in these trials. The recipient’s movement and future target stay in place. This deliberately mismatched input tests whether knowing the correct moment helped. It can draw a later donor during offline scoring.

The correct patches gave 0.38191° of error and the shifted patches 0.38147°. That difference is under half a thousandth of a degree. The wider gap is between using both patches and using motion alone (0.36132°). Neither patch alone closed it. The same ordering appeared during development, where the specialist missed its prespecified criterion.

Results · The shared movement encoder4 / 5

What travels from a wrist or a rat to a sensor on a back?

NeuroTranslate 0.0 already had a set of weights for reading motion across time. Earlier studies trained it on distances travelled by a human wrist and a tracked rat. Here we kept that part fixed and fitted a new readout for the back sensor’s angle. Only the weights travelled between studies. The people, animals, tasks and measurements belonged to their own experiments. Shared forecast and methods [4]

Four 25 ms pieces describe net turn, angular path length and the variation and successive changes in each patch’s raw voltage. The readout gets these features, the recent turn and the current task. The shared model also gets the encoder’s representation. We compared it with a linear readout alone and with an identical encoder left at one random initialization. All three readouts learned from the same training people.

For the wrong-patch comparison, the donor comes from the other trial of the same person doing the same task, at the corresponding relative position in its list of eligible times. The model and the recipient’s movement stay fixed. The specialist on page 3 uses a different donor rule.

With both patches, the frozen encoder missed by 0.38495° on average. Swapping the patches raised error to 0.38530°. Both the linear readout (0.38007°) and the random encoder (0.38153°) came closer than the pretrained full model. The earlier weights offered no average advantage over these comparisons on this subset. The four-person development criterion also failed.

Interpretation and sources5 / 5

What remains open?

The six verified trials yielded 1,152 standing and 1,152 walking intervals. Typical angles differed sharply. Across the three people, the median of their median observed turns was 0.186° standing and 1.664° walking. Each person contributed many measurements, but the comparison still has only three independent people. All three had lower shared-model error without the patches. Two had lower error with the correct patches than with the other trial’s patches. Even if all three paired differences point the same way, an exact two-sided sign test can give a p-value no smaller than 0.25.

Before scoring, we required the full specialist to beat its best simple or motion-only forecast by 5% and the shifted patches by 2%. The shared model had to beat motion alone and swapped patches by 5% each. Each family also needed improvement on both comparisons in at least three of four people and a task-by-task check for worsening. Six usable trials allow a description of the observed errors. They leave both four-person criteria unassessed.

What the electrodes can tell us

The patch locations tell us where the contacts sat. Voltage there can include muscle activity, heartbeat and changes in skin contact as a person moves. The forecasts use only preceding raw samples. Filtering forward and backward or choosing channels from a whole-trial artifact map could let a later measurement influence the past. Neither the sensor angle nor a successful electrical forecast would identify a voltage generator, a particular joint motion or a person’s intention.

We can test another person against the same question because their sensor supplies the answer. To ask about the twist of a particular joint would require independent measurements of the segments on either side. A step into a turn or a change of instruction would require testing windows that include those transitions.
