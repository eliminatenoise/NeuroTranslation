Forecasting movement on first sight of a new body

Optical movement study · 21 November 2026

A camera following a mouse and a camera following a worm can both tell us where the animal was a moment ago. Can the same model use those positions to tell us where its visible parts will go next, without asking it to perform a calibration movement?

We trained a forecaster on a mouse recording and a gerbil recording. It received the positions of tracked points and their times, with no species name, fixed skeleton or individual fit. Across 13 held recordings near 120 ms, its mean error was 0.1320 of the visible landmarks’ radius. Leaving each point where it was gave 0.1690. A slower locust recording required a separate test at 200 ms.

Adding a fly and two worm recordings raised short-horizon error from 0.1397 to 0.1563 on the ten recordings common to both tests. It lowered error from 0.1608 to 0.1464 on the one locust recording. This is evidence about these animals, cameras and training weights. Human first use remains untested.

Six species, sixteen recordings

The inputs span three phyla, or major branches of animal life. The camera sees jointed limbs and tails in rodents, head and body landmarks in insects, and a bending centreline in worms. Even the same species can present the model with quite different points.

What each fit had seen

The primary fit used two recordings, nine tracks and 13,816 training point examples. The held set comprised one separate mouse recording, four insect recordings and nine worm recordings. Both invertebrate phyla were absent from training.

The secondary fit added the single fly and one recording each of N2 and long-bodied worms. This gave five recordings, twelve tracks and 32,763 examples. All three dumpy-worm recordings remained held. The shared test now contained eleven recordings spanning three phyla already represented in training.

We kept every physical recording intact when assigning training and test sets. Each phylum received equal sampling probability in training, divided among its recordings and then its tracks. The added fly alone received one third of the mixed fit’s training weight. The contrast changes both the animals sampled and their weights.

NeuroTranslation · Body shape 3 / 6

Which parts of a body can a camera measure?

Nine worm recordings supply both a centreline and an outline in micrometres. Joining 49 points from head to tail gives a projected length. Dividing outline area by that length gives a mean projected width, averaged across the tapered head, body and tail.

CB1282 carries dpy-20(e1282) IV and CB678 carries lon-2(e678) X. The long-strain recordings had narrower projected bodies than the dumpy-strain recordings. The N2 length range overlapped the long strain. Each range describes three recording medians. There is no cross-day animal identity or independent measure of mass, girth or three-dimensional length.

A tail changes what “length” means

In open-field mice, a path from nose to tail tip measured 126–142 mm across track medians using the source’s nominal camera scale. Gerbil nose-to-tail paths spanned 83–185 mm in a home cage containing two pups and two adults. The gerbil source gives ages for the group. It does not establish an age for each of these four measurements.

In flies, a head–thorax–abdomen path measured 2.21–2.68 mm across both layouts. A bee path measured 16.4–18.9 mm. For locusts the greatest separation of visible points measured 155–184 pixels across track medians. The arena diameter alone cannot supply each animal’s physical length. Camera angle, posture and which parts remain visible affect every comparison. These whole-record descriptions were kept outside fitting and prediction.

NeuroTranslation · First use 4 / 6

How soon can a prediction begin?

On the first visible frame the software can already return a position. Its rule is simply to leave each visible point where it is. An unseen point stays unknown. Of 21 original test tracks, 17 showed at least one point on the first frame. Four began with an empty view.

Learning enters the forecast after five naturally observed frames. The weights stay fixed as the observed history changes. A point must be visible now and appear at least three times within that window. Its neighbors contribute evidence about how the animal is moving. No calibration gesture, species entry or retraining on the new recording is required.

Time spent computing

In five fresh Python processes on an AMD Ryzen AI Max+ 395 CPU, the median import took 35.3 ms and loading weights took 0.35 ms. Given five frames of seven fictional points, the first learned forecast took 0.81 ms and a warmed forecast took 0.193 ms (median). File caches were uncontrolled. Camera acquisition, landmark detection, identity assignment and data transport were outside these measurements.

NeuroTranslation · Results 5 / 6

Where does a small average hide a large miss?

For each visible point we measured the distance between its forecast and the later optical position. We divided by the root-mean-square distance of the eligible points from their centre at the forecast instant. An error of 0.10 is one tenth of this projected radius. 23,535 scored point endpoints for all 13 short-horizon recordings, 17,943 on the shared ten and 1,680 on the locust recording. The held locusts share a single clip. The actual short-horizon target lay 86.7–140 ms ahead. We kept the two horizons separate.

For a given point the model weighs six possibilities, including staying still, following the point’s own velocity, or following the surrounding points. The geometry inputs describe landmark spread, relative distances and neighboring motion in the camera plane. Zeroing these inputs during both training and testing reduced mixed-fit error on the shared ten, from 0.1563 to 0.1490. A rigid translation and rotation of the observed configuration scored 0.1570.

The recording matters

Seven of the ten shared short recordings were worms. Giving each phylum equal weight raised mammal-fit error to 0.2156 and mixed-fit error to 0.2446. The largest mammal-fit error was 0.4147 on the bee recording (52.0 pixels). For the mixed fit it was 0.5197 on the paired flies (20.7 pixels). Pixels have a different physical meaning in the two views.

Of 624 scheduled forecast instants on the shared short test, 587 had a jointly scorable target. Randomly hiding 20% of previously visible inputs cut matched endpoints from 17,943 to 13,237, and from 1,680 to 1,308 for the locust. A nominal 90% error circle, sized using held-out training recordings, covered 90.5% on average across the ten natural mixed-fit recordings. Individual coverage ranged from 53.0% on paired flies to 100% on several worms. A new camera or animal has no guaranteed coverage.

NeuroTranslation · Interpretation and evidence 6 / 6

What transfers to a new user?

What transferred here was a short forecast in a camera plane. An animal supplied a recording. It received no signal or intervention. The model can reuse its weights across different numbers of points, provided their identities persist within the observed sequence. A worm centreline index may follow a relative position along the body while the tissue beneath it moves.

Each rodent or insect layout came from a single recording. Replication across sites with the same layout was unavailable. A mouse changed camera view, strain and setting together. Differences between species also carry differences in cameras, landmarks, behavior and tracking errors. Multiple points from the same animal do not supply independent replications, and optical target error was never independently measured. A human first-use test would need independent participants with varied builds, views and sensor layouts. Clinical benefit, intent, force and three-dimensional motion remain untested.

From optical tracks to vertebrate neural data

Neural transfer poses a separate question. The next study calls for a wide range of directly recorded neural datasets across vertebrate clades and classes, with recording sites, tasks and measurement methods identified. Holding out entire lineages, individuals and sites where the data permit would test which predictions survive those changes. This optical pilot contains no neural recordings from any species.

Reproducing the forecasts

The released model includes the frozen protocol, two sets of weights, native-unit results and a source downloader. Each fit used the same seed (1729), 192 Adam updates, batches of 512 and a 32-unit hidden layer. An independent refit reproduced both sets of weights.
