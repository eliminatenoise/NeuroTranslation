What does the arm cue already tell us?

Tell a model which movement the picture cued and it can make a forecast before it reads a single sensor. How much of that forecast survives contact with the arm? And how much does an electrode add once the model already knows what the person was asked to do?

Patrick Ofner and colleagues recorded a seated participant’s supported right arm as pictures prompted six movements and rest. In one analysis we forecast the elbow reading after a bend or straighten cue. Adding scalp voltage at C3 made the average forecast worse. In a second analysis we forecast three instrument readings for all seven cues. Telling a shared model the correct cue helped in every run. A simpler forecast based on the cue alone had the lowest average error.

What the instruments follow

The support measured the elbow and forearm rotation. A glove measured the finger joint nearest the hand on the index finger. The scalp cap measured electrical activity at 61 sites, with three additional channels around the eyes. A cue marks the requested action. A later change in a sensor is evidence of what moved. Those two events can fall at different times.

512 readings per second. Scalp and eye channels follow the event clock.

64 readings per second. The support and glove supply one value every eight event ticks. At any cutoff we use the latest available reading.

0.25 seconds after the cue. Both forecasts stop taking input here. Every target comes later.

All three runs came from the same person, S1, performing the movements. Each run contained six repetitions of each cue. The 36 elbow cues are part of the 126 trials in the wider study. The three folds reuse training runs and the two studies overlap. One run-1 flexion example was known when the protocols were fixed. This is a within-person development comparison. A forecast for a new person would require a different test.

Does the right trial’s scalp signal help?

Start with 18 bend cues and 18 straighten cues. Predict how far the elbow reading will change from a quarter second to one second after the cue. Each endpoint is the median of the eight latest sensor readings. The cue and the four most recent elbow medians supply a baseline. A ridge fit learns the relation on two runs while penalising large coefficients. Each test run contains 12 trials, six per cue. The other 24 determine the fit and feature scales.

Add one electrode, C3, over the left side of the scalp. Four consecutive 125-millisecond windows end at the cutoff. Each supplies a mean, and the last three means enter the fit as changes from the first. We repeat the fit with the average of the three eye channels. Then we keep the original C3 model and give it the next trial’s C3 windows from the same cue and held run, cycling through all six trials. The recipient keeps its own cue, elbow and target.

Figure 1. Mean absolute error gives each of six run-by-cue groups equal weight (6 trials each, 36 total). “No change” predicts zero displacement. “Cue median” predicts the fitting runs’ median displacement for that cue.

The eye control was easier to beat than a misplaced C3 signal

C3 beat the eye replacement. It lost to both the cue-and-elbow fit and its own wrong-trial control. C3 improved on the cue-and-elbow fit in one of three runs and in neither cue group. The prespecified test required both groups, at least two runs and lower error than all three comparators. It failed. Of 36 measured changes, 25 had magnitude at most 0.1 native units and 17 had the opposite sign to their fitting-set cue median.

A secondary fit gave the four C3 windows different weights according to their similarity to the last window, with a fixed preference for recent readings. Its average was 0.07122, just below the 0.07237 cue-and-elbow baseline. This exploratory scalar attention scheme has no learned encoder or pretrained weights. Its error rose slightly for bend cues and fell for straighten cues relative to the baseline. The small pooled difference needs a fresh test. Explore the two elbow cues

The same arm history, a different instruction

Keep the early elbow, forearm and index-finger readings fixed. Tell the model that the picture meant “open the hand”, then give it a different label. We can score both forecasts against the very same movement. The seven categories cover elbow flexion and extension, turning the palm up and down, closing and opening the hand, and rest. The model receives the category assigned to the picture. The original wording and the participant’s private intention are unknown.

We fitted on 84 trials and scored 42 in each held run. All 126 qualified. Four 125-millisecond bins supply each instrument’s median level and within-bin change. The medians of eight readings in three later windows, ending 0.5, 1.0 and 1.5 seconds after the cutoff, supply target changes from the last input level. A previously trained NeuroTranslate encoder, one block with 24 features per bin, transforms the four bins into extra features. Its weights stay fixed. A linear readout fitted on the two training runs combines them with the original history and, when available, the cue. Its motion history comes entirely from the support and glove.

Which reading missed, and by how much?

The combined score lets a small finger-sensor change count alongside a large forearm-sensor change. To see what a miss means on either instrument, go back to its stored scale. What would it take to read the next arm?

The task schedule gave the model real information before it saw the movement. A known category alone had the lowest combined error across these runs, although holding still won in run 1. This comparison establishes the value of supplying a label to this model on these recordings. It cannot tell us whether a model can discover an unspoken instruction, or where a signal recorded at C3 originated.

The stored unit fields are blank. A rising value means an increase on the instrument’s scale. Interpreting that increase as degrees, or assigning a physical direction, requires calibration. Rounding a cutoff down to the 64 Hz grid makes a mechanical sample up to 7/512 s old. Display delay, hardware synchronisation and movement onset remain unmeasured here. A new participant kept entirely outside fitting would supply the missing test of transfer.
