What can the shin tell us about the next pedal reading?

NeuroTranslation research supplement · AnkleImage Task 1 · 21 November 2026

Pull upward against a fixed pedal. The pedal stays put, but its load changes. How much can an electrode over the shin tell us about that change once we know what the pedal has just done?

We tested two forecasting models on recordings of seated ankle contractions. Each predicted how far the load-cell reading would rise or fall over the next 100 positions on the trial’s nominal 1 kHz series. The simplest moving forecast takes the change over the last 100 positions and predicts that it will happen again.

For the participant in Figure 1, matching the muscle signal reduced specialist error relative to both the load-and-setting model and the other-trial control. The shared encoder gave its lowest error among those three with load and setting alone. Only this one participant supplied both complete trial pairs. One unusable neutral trial from the second participant leaves the planned two-person comparison unanswered.

A reading now, a reading later

A seated volunteer tried to lift the forefoot against a secured pedal. Surface electrodes measured electrical activity over the tibialis anterior, the muscle along the front of the shin. This is surface electromyography (EMG). The load cell measured the mechanical response at the pedal. The two signals share a row in the recording. No brain signal, nerve recording or ultrasound image enters this forecast.

What the clocks establish

Zhang and colleagues report sampling both channels simultaneously at 1,000 samples per second. That makes the forecast nominally 100 milliseconds. A01’s exports include regular elapsed-time labels. A02’s have no time vector. Counting rows retains their order but cannot establish the true duration of every interval or rule out a dropped sample. All four files from one reserved participant preserve regular time labels. In three files from the other, we verified the authors’ 1 kHz exports against every tenth observation on the stored 10 kHz grid. In these files our 100 positions refer to the authors’ 1 kHz series. The recordings do not establish the physical delay between electrical activity and pedal loading.

Who entered each test

After reviewing one neutral Task 1 recording for eligibility, we used A01 and A02 to develop the models. Each served once as the person withheld during development. We then fitted the models on both people and froze the weights before assessing the two reserved participants. The plan specified trials 1 and 2 for every person at the archive settings angle0 and angle5. These codes describe the apparatus conditions. The second label alone does not establish a signed anatomical angle. The selected load channel is named “right” in the export. The measured limb’s side cannot be inferred from that channel name.

The same four participant codes occur in an earlier Task 3 ultrasound analysis. Those were different contractions. No time point or image from Task 3 was paired with a Task 1 reading.

Three ways to forecast a change

Let the load approach an equilibrium

Suppose a pedal reading tends towards a level set by the current contraction. Write L for the present reading and E for that estimated equilibrium. A first-order relaxation predicts a change of (1 − exp(−100/150)) × (E − L), about 49% of the remaining difference. One fit estimated the equilibrium from pedal setting alone. A second also used two smoothed histories of the muscle signal’s magnitude and their recent changes. Their smoothing constants were 25 and 80 nominal sample intervals. We chose all three constants for the model. None measures a physiological time constant.

Reuse a movement model

The shared NeuroTranslate 0.0 encoder summarizes an input history using weights learned previously. We kept those weights fixed. Each of four 50-interval bins supplied three descriptions of the pedal trace and three of muscle activity. A fitted linear readout combined those values, recent load change, setting and the encoder’s final 24 numbers to correct the repeat-change forecast. Leaving out the encoder tested what a direct linear correction could do. A randomly initialized encoder tested how much the previously learned weights contributed.

Learn from the pedal trials

A specialist Transformer learned to compare ten consecutive 20-interval bins. Each bin summarized load level and change, setting, muscle-signal level and variation, and how that variation coincided with a change in load. Two attention heads weighted the ten bins. A linear output from the final bin produced the forecast. Training the 1,525-parameter model took 96 full-batch updates per fit. Input and output scales came from the training people alone.

Put the other contraction through the same fit

For each family, we kept the fitted model, pedal history, setting and target fixed, then swapped the complete EMG history with one from the person’s other trial at that setting. The specialist took the same cutoff index. The shared model took the corresponding fraction of the other trial, rounding down its cutoff rank. Both recalculated the features. The swap tests whether the model uses this particular pairing. It draws on a second completed trial, so it is an offline control.

Which records allow a comparison?

For the participant with incomplete data, the first neutral trial contains two columns under the right-load name. It lacks both a verified time vector and a single-channel 1 kHz author export. Neither column could supply the specified endpoint. We retained trial 2 as a single descriptive series. Its missing partner rules out the swapped-EMG control. This participant’s three usable series required the source-field check described on page 2 after the fits were frozen.

In development, repeating recent change gave the lowest error (0.0615). The matched specialist scored 0.0749. Across the four-trial set, the specialist with matched EMG edged ahead of recent change (0.0545 versus 0.0563). On the other person’s angle5 pair, the matched trace helped relative to the swapped one, yet the model using load and setting alone was more accurate (0.1201 versus 0.1670). The load-and-setting fit also had lower error on the remaining neutral trial.

What would the electrode have to add?

The specialist had to reduce mean error by at least 5% against the best prespecified forecast without EMG and 2% against the swapped trace. It also had to beat both its load-and-setting fit and the swapped trace for each person, with neither setting worse on average. The development specialist met the 2% swapped-trace and setting requirements. It missed the 5% comparison with repeating recent change and failed to improve on both controls in each individual.

The shared model had to reduce mean error by at least 5% against each of its load-and-setting and swapped-EMG controls, with gains over both in each person. One of the two development participants improved over both controls. Neither family met its development criterion. The unusable neutral trial prevents both prespecified held assessments. No pooled two-person result can answer either test.

There were 624 development windows. Of the 446 reserved windows, 272 came from the four-trial set. The other person supplied 116 at angle5 and 58 in the single neutral trial. The first two sets supplied all 388 windows with a matched other-trial control. Cutoffs advanced 100 positions on the selected series at a time. Two adjacent histories therefore shared 101 observations, and neighboring forecast intervals shared a boundary reading. Two people entered development and only one of the two reserved people contributed both complete trial pairs. Repeating windows gives a more detailed view of each individual. It cannot establish a population benefit or estimate its uncertainty reliably.

When could the pedal give a torque?

A calibrated force applied perpendicular to the apparatus arm would give a moment of F × 0.1 m. For example, a hypothetical 10 N perpendicular force would give 1 N·m. The authors describe calibration with known weights, but the selected exports and accompanying material do not supply a verified signed force gain and zero for this analysis. That example is arithmetic. The observed errors stay in native load-channel units. Even a calibrated net ankle moment would combine contributions from structures around the joint. Neither this forecast nor its timing identifies individual muscle force, neural commands or a therapeutic effect.
