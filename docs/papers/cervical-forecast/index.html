Can a recording at the armpit improve a forecast at the neck?

@eliminatenoise · November 2026

Eight milliseconds after a stimulus at the wrist, the model already knows the voltage at the neck. What does the recording at the armpit add?

Each forecast concerns a single stimulus in an existing experiment. We ask how much the average neck voltage will change from before that stimulus to 12–14 milliseconds afterward. A forecast that uses the armpit reading has two comparisons to win. It should do better than a model that never sees this reading and better than one given the armpit signal from the wrong event. Across six people held out from fitting (480 events), neither model cleared both comparisons. For the Transformer, a wrong-event armpit reading gave a smaller average error than the correct one. For the robust regression, leaving the armpit channel out altogether worked better.

What the electrodes measured

A surface electrode records a voltage difference between two sites. Here the neck channel, SC6, measures the voltage over the sixth cervical vertebra relative to a second electrode over the sixth thoracic vertebra, TH6, farther down the back. A change at either site alters the reading. A microvolt (µV) is one millionth of a volt.

The original experiment recorded the electrical response to stimulation of the left median nerve at the wrist. The channel named Biceps recorded at the left armpit (axilla) over the biceps, relative to a nearby electrode about 3 cm closer to the shoulder. The investigators used this pair to measure peripheral nerve activity. The muscle recording came from a different pair over the base of the thumb and a nearby reference.

A scalp electrode, CP4, used the right mastoid behind the ear as its reference. The heart channel (ECG) used the lower left rib margin and a reference below the right collarbone. These channels shared a clock. They did not share a single electrical reference. Nierula et al., recording methods

Measurements and models2 / 5

Four milliseconds between input and target

Zero is the recorded stimulus marker. The instruments sampled 10,000 times a second. We kept the original acquisition filters and applied no additional signal filter. The right endpoint of each interval is excluded. The four-millisecond lead is measured on stored recordings. End-to-end processing time on a live instrument was never tested.

12 to 14 msTwenty later neck samples, kept for the answer. The target is their mean minus the baseline, in µV.

The most recent neck value makes a forecast on its own. Average the 7–8 ms neck reading and subtract the baseline. Predict that this value will persist. Its error against the target is simply the difference between the early and later readings because the same baseline cancels. This gives the more complicated models a demanding comparison.

Two ways to use the armpit recording

The Transformer groups the voltage samples into half-millisecond averages. Each of its 16 time positions carries readings from neck, thumb, scalp and heart, plus the event’s position in the run. One version adds six early armpit averages. Neither version sees the pre-stimulus armpit reading. The network learns which time positions to combine through a single layer of attention, then averages their representations to forecast one voltage change.

The robust regression begins with the 7–8 ms persistence forecast. It learns how much to add to that value. It takes five pre-stimulus and three early one-millisecond averages from each included channel, subtracting each channel’s own pre-stimulus mean. Its fit gives unusually large errors less weight. Armpit inputs here include the five centered pre-stimulus bins as well as the three early bins.

Ridge regression predicts a weighted sum of the same inputs, penalizing large weights to limit sensitivity to the fitting data. It and the Transformer use training-set means and standard deviations. The robust fit uses training medians and scaled median absolute deviations. Repeated events from a test person never enter those scaling estimates. Technical settings appear on page 5.

Forecast performance3 / 5

Which forecast came closest?

For development, three people supplied 240 training events and the fourth supplied 80 test events. We took turns holding each person out. We then fitted the models to all four and fixed their weights and scaling before assessing six separate people. Each contributed the first 80 eligible chronological median-nerve events in one run. The rule picked run 03 where available, otherwise the lowest available run. No events were removed for their measured values.

During development the Transformer fell from 19.70 to 18.20 µV MAE when it received the armpit channel. Yet a network trained and tested on wrong-event armpit signals scored 16.99 µV. For the robust model the correctly paired channel reduced error by 0.22 µV against wrong events, while increasing it by 0.42 µV against omission.

On the six new people, holding the recent neck voltage constant missed by 8.09 µV on average. The correctly paired Transformer missed by 23.28 µV, exceeding even the 22.15 µV error from predicting the training mean every time. With identical input windows, ridge regression had much smaller errors than the Transformer in both stages. The robust regression using neck, thumb, scalp and heart had the smallest assessment MAE in the table (7.77 µV). Adding the armpit increased it to 9.65 µV.

Event pairing and uncertainty4 / 5

Does the recording belong to this event?

Keep the neck, thumb, scalp and heart readings in place and swap the armpit trace between events in the same person. If the correct pairing matters, the error should increase. The development Transformer’s comparison trained and scored a separate network with the armpit reading from 37 events earlier, wrapping around its 80-event run. The ridge control used the same shift. This tells us how well a model can learn and use the consistently mismatched inputs.

The six-person Transformer comparison instead kept the correctly trained weights fixed and supplied the six early armpit bins from 37 events later at scoring only. The robust model used this same later-event rule in both stages and swapped all eight centered armpit bins, leaving the neck anchor intact. Each swap is a predetermined diagnostic. A single shift cannot give a distribution of chance results.

The Transformer required positive average gains against both controls and improvement on both in at least 3 of 4 development people or 5 of 6 new people. It improved on both for 2 of 4 and 1 of 6. The robust model required at least 5% lower mean error against both and improvement on both in every person. It achieved the paired improvements for 1 of 4 and 3 of 6. Neither passed in either stage.

An exact two-sided sign test counts people with positive versus negative gains, discarding ties. It asks how unusual the count would be if either direction were equally likely. On the six new people, the Transformer’s tests against omission and wrong pairing each gave p = 0.6875. The corresponding robust-model values were 1 and 0.21875. Six people give little precision even with 480 events. Positive and negative differences in both stages leave the size of any reproducible benefit uncertain.

Interpretation and reproducibility5 / 5

What was the later neck reading?

In the original experiment, Nierula and colleagues identified a negative peak near 13 ms, called N13. They corrected stimulus and heartbeat artifacts, filtered the recording, rejected trials, and averaged 1,210–2,000 median-nerve trials per person. With a thoracic reference they found a mean peak of −0.85 ± 0.05 µV at 13.25 ± 0.18 ms across 36 people (means ± standard errors). They used each person’s peak and a −110 to −10 ms baseline. Original Table 1 · Figure 3

An earlier NeuroTranslation pilot averaged eight raw events in one person, with a fixed 12–14 ms window and a −15 to −13 ms baseline. It yielded +1.70225 µV. Those eight events overlap development. The sign belongs to that raw average. The present single-event forecasts use a different baseline, and none of these forecast errors measures the height or timing of N13.

Carrying the neck voltage forward gives a close forecast whenever the measured voltage changes little over the next few milliseconds. Several sources could contribute to that persistence, including stimulus-related voltage, heart activity, nearby muscles, drift and signals reaching both electrodes. These results leave the source and direction of transmission unresolved. All ten people came from one acquisition study. Generalization to another laboratory, movement or clinical condition remains untested.

How the fitted comparisons can be reproduced

The Transformer uses 16 learned positions, width 16, two attention heads and a 16→32→16 feedforward block. It adds the zero-based event number divided by 79 at every position. With seed 1701, AdamW ran 100 full-batch updates (learning rate 0.003, weight decay 0.01), using squared error, no dropout or early stopping. Ridge penalizes squared coefficients by 32 and leaves its intercept free. The robust fit clips standardized features at ±8, uses Huber threshold 1.345, coefficient penalty 0.08 and 30 reweighted least-squares updates. All fits retained the original test errors, including large errors.
