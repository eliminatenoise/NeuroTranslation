NeuroTranslation · experimental research report

The same electrodes,
one day later

A robot pushes a handle in a macaque’s hand. Can the last fifth of a second of movement tell us how much electrical activity comes next in the sensory cortex?

We trained on one day and tested on the next. The two recordings come from the same animal. At 26 electrode contacts, both days contain counts of electrical events that the original investigators left unsorted. An individual contact can pick up signals from more than one cell.

What the second day showed

On 2,436 matched windows from 609 second-day push trials, the model given movement, counts and cues had mean deviance 1.9875 per contact. Counts and cues alone gave 1.9901. Replacing the recorded movement with movement from an earlier matched trial gave 1.9907. The two comparisons did not jointly meet the prespecified criterion for a movement benefit.

Methods · recordings and timing

Count the events after the cutoff

Chowdhury, Glaser and Miller recorded reaches in a rhesus macaque. The animal held a handle that moved in a plane. The apparatus could briefly push it. Later, a target and a go cue prompted the animal to reach. The public recordings contain the handle’s position, optical measurements from the arm and counts of electrical events detected in area 2, part of the brain that receives information about the body. Our count forecasts use the handle measurements. The companion atlas shows the arm markers from the same recording. Original data · Original study

Every 10 milliseconds the source gives us a position and a count at each electrode. The model reads 20 preceding bins, in five groups of four. The movement input in each group is the last measured handle position in centimetres and the change from its first sample. The count input sums the four bins at each contact. A cue enters only after its recorded time. At the exact start of a push, the model has yet to see that event.

At each cutoff, the target is the next ten bins summed separately at each of the 26 contacts. We forecast at 0, 50, 100 and 150 milliseconds after an event. A late forecast after a push may include the animal’s corrective response. Each window, its neighbours and the original trial stay together throughout scoring.

Which trials enter

The training group was the first 324 rewarded originals from September 12, 2017. Two trials had incompatible cue timing, leaving 322 originals and 1,288 pre-target push windows. The next 108 rewarded originals supplied a same-day validation comparison. All fitted means, scales and model weights come from the earlier training group. The final 108 same-day trials had been used in an earlier handle-displacement study and stayed outside this one.

On September 13, one original trial had already been inspected while checking the recording. We excluded it. The remaining trial identities, contact numbers and eligible time windows were fixed from the task records before the models were evaluated. The post-check totals were 2,452 windows for the pre-target push, 2,444 windows for a reach cue after a push, 2,652 windows for a reach cue without a push.

Methods · models and controls

Give movement a fair comparison

A model can forecast future activity using recent activity alone. A visible cue may also identify what comes next. We compared a model with all three inputs against one given the earlier electrical counts and available cues. A second comparison replaced the full model’s movement input with a movement recorded in an earlier, different trial. The donor had to match the event type, its direction, the offset and the visible cue pattern across the five input groups.

The six input combinations use the same compact Transformer architecture and training budget. The network projects each of five time samples into 16 numbers. Its attention layer learns how much weight to give each sample. The output gives 26 positive expected counts. Each combination is fitted three times from different initial weights. We calculate the error for each fit before averaging the three errors. We also fit six count regressions to the same inputs. These combine the five sets of measurements linearly on a log scale, with a penalty on large coefficients. Code and parameters

Two simpler forecasts set the scale. The first predicts the first day’s mean for each electrode. The second repeats the count from the immediately preceding 100 milliseconds. A forecast of 1.4 events means an average of 1.4 over repetitions. Any observed trial gives a whole number.

How error is counted

For an observation y and expected count m, Poisson deviance is 2 × [y log(y/m) − y + m]. Smaller values are better. For scoring we set a floor of one millionth of an event on the expected count. An observed zero contributes no logarithmic term. A separate absolute-error measure reports the average miss in events per contact per 100 milliseconds.

We average across contacts and windows within a trial first, then give each contributing trial equal weight. We form uncertainty intervals by sampling blocks of 12 consecutive phase-eligible original trials, 5,000 times. The primary test requires a lower deviance on the new day against both the counts-and-cues model and the matched wrong-movement control. The upper endpoint of each paired 95% interval must fall below zero.

What this recording can resolve

The original study asked how the brain represents the movement of the whole arm. Here the target is specifically the detected, unsorted activity recorded at contacts with matching labels on both days. Sorting, electrode drift and day-to-day changes in the populations near an electrode can all affect those counts. The source contains no raw voltage or cross-day cell tracking. It supplies previously filtered movement traces, which limits claims about live response time. These data do not establish a sensory latency, a causal pathway or performance in another animal.

The companion handle study asks the reverse question, how well previous movement and spikes forecast later handle displacement within one day. The numerical units and outcomes differ. The model archive includes each fitted input combination and the scores by contact, event direction and offset.
