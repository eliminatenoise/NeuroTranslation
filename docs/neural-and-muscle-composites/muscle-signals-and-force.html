Neural signals, muscle and movement

What can tell us how hard a muscle pulls?

Hold an empty mug still, then fill it with water and hold it in the same place. A camera can give the mug the same position in both recordings. Your hand has to support more weight in the second. Even if we know the weight, several muscles can share the effort, and muscles on opposite sides of a joint can pull at the same time. More precise motion capture can improve the path we measure. The path alone leaves more than one possible answer for the pull of each muscle. How a musculoskeletal model estimates individual muscle forces

A muscle composite brings together observations about a muscle during the same task. Skin electrodes record tiny changes in voltage as muscle fibres become electrically excited. This recording is called electromyography, or EMG. It can tell us when a muscle becomes active. It can also help estimate force if the electrical signal and the specified force have been measured together under comparable loads and contraction conditions. In experiments with the upper arm, shoulder and hand, the relationship between EMG and force depended on which muscle the researchers tested. Shortening speed matters too. A force estimate needs its own check when those conditions change. Lawrence and De Luca’s force measurements · Bigland and Lippold’s shortening experiments

Contractile state describes the muscle’s mechanics. How hard is it pulling? How long are its fibres, and how fast are they shortening or lengthening? Each question takes a different measurement. During treadmill walking, Fukunaga and colleagues used ultrasound to follow bundles of calf muscle fibres, called fascicles. The bundles kept nearly the same length during stance. The researchers combined the images with joint motion and anatomy to estimate that the tendon stretched about 7 mm during single-leg support. The push measured at the floor was another quantity again. A simulation can estimate the pull in a particular muscle, and that estimate needs an independent comparison at the same anatomical level. Fukunaga and colleagues’ walking experiment

Some information travels back from the body. A sensory afferent is a nerve fibre that carries a signal toward the spinal cord or brain. In a block-grasping experiment, researchers recorded impulses in sensory fibres serving hand muscles while also measuring finger motion and EMG. The recorded fibres had their own firing patterns. A camera can show a finger closing around the block. To learn what a sensory fibre signalled, we need the nerve recording or a prediction checked against one. If both the neural and muscle composites use the same EMG trace, it counts as one recording. Whether combining those composites improves a prediction is a question for held-out trials. Two datasets to explore

  • In AnkleImage, compare the shin muscle’s electrical activity with its ultrasound images as the participant lifts the front of the foot. The fixed-angle pedal trials also measured force at the pedal. The pedal receives the combined action of the muscles around the ankle. For comparisons of timing, verify how each exported image frame maps to the electrical samples. AnkleImage data · Study and recording methods
  • In Kim and colleagues’ walking dataset, the same walking trials include shin ultrasound, muscle electrical recordings, tracked body motion and floor forces. A force plate measures the foot’s interaction with the floor. A model that predicts this force can be checked against the plate. A claim about one muscle’s tension needs a reference for that muscle.
