What does the forearm add?

Turning a key and folding a sheet of paper put different demands on the wrist. One model knows the activity and the time within it. The other also receives half a second of surface electromyography (EMG) from seven forearm electrodes. Both estimate the wrist angle a quarter of a second later on the recording clock.

How close were the estimates?

Fourteen people supplied training movements in the KIN-MUS UJI study. Across 104 trials of 26 activities from four different people, these released models had a two-axis error of 36.165° from task and time alone and 34.700° with forearm EMG. Flexion–extension error fell, while side-to-side error rose. Four final test participants remain unevaluated.

The weighted combination was then learned using only the 14 training people. On the same four development participants, it scored 34.971°. Relative to using the full EMG adjustment, side-to-side error fell by 0.312° and flexion–extension error rose by 0.512°.

The glove angles used for scoring were filtered forwards and backwards before release. Both models therefore exclude earlier glove angles from their inputs. The 250 ms interval refers to stored timestamps.
