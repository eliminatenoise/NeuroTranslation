How close was the wrist forecast?

As people lifted a 12 kg box, cameras tracked both wrists. Each fitted model saw 240 milliseconds of wrist motion and forecast where the right wrist would be 100 milliseconds later.

How the forecasts were measured

Each person contributed one recording of repeated crouch lifts. Cameras followed marks on the skin of both wrists, plates underfoot measured floor forces, and six muscle recordings picked up electrical activity from the shoulders and upper back.

Both methods learned from 2,316 windows of movement from seven people. The linear model (ridge) learned fixed weights for the readings. The attention model learned how to give different readings different weight. In each window, the positions of both wrists were recorded 25 times over 240 milliseconds.

The test used six other people. Of 2,402 scheduled windows, 17 lacked a usable history. In the remaining 2,385, every forecast could be compared with the right wrist’s measured position 100 milliseconds later. The study measured the straight-line distance in three dimensions, averaged the misses for each person, then gave all six people equal weight.

The study tested whether adding current muscle readings to wrist positions and floor forces consistently helped the attention model. Error fell for two people, rose for four and tied for none. The exact two-sided sign test gave p = 0.6875. The other comparisons describe the same recorded task.

Using muscle readings from one second earlier also changes which part of a lift they can come from. This checks timing. The comparisons alone cannot say whether muscle activity caused a change in the wrist’s path.

Alexander K. Beattie, Matti J. Kortelainen, Paavo Vartiainen and Pasi A. Karjalainen recorded the Kuopio Full-Body Dataset (v1.2.1, CC BY 4.0). The NeuroTranslation study computed these errors from its crouch lifts.
