Pelvis marker studies

Can floor force help predict a squat?

As you rise from a squat, the camera tracks how far your hips move. Plates under your feet record how hard you push into the floor. We asked whether that second measurement helps a model predict the next 100 milliseconds of a squat, including moments when an original camera reading goes missing.

Adding floor force to a simple linear model reduced its error from 9.60 to 6.60 mm for the two markers on the back of the pelvis when a reading was missing. For the four-marker average in those windows, continuing the recent speed did best at 4.16 mm.

What the sensors add

The linear model with camera and floor force had the smallest overall error on both targets. Adding the thigh signals to that model raised its overall error. The attention models, which learn how to weigh different points in the history, had higher error than the linear models under the same input conditions overall.

The thigh electrodes record electrical activity at the skin. The plates record force at the floor. A muscle’s own force would need a different measurement or a model tested against one. Here the floor and electrode readings were used to predict movement, with the later camera readings as the test.

Eight of twelve previously studied people provided comparable data. Three recordings had missing or unresolved electrode identities and one had no comparable window with a measured endpoint. The result describes one deep-squat task in one lab. The comparison had no scoreable cases where none of the visible markers repeated across the final two camera readings.

How the comparison worked

Five camera instants span the 200 milliseconds before the forecast through the issue time. Each floor or thigh reading summarizes the 50 milliseconds before its camera instant. We used upward force from two plates and the root mean square of the stored voltage over six front-thigh electrodes. A 10 millisecond block of exact zeros made the corresponding electrode history unavailable. The recorded clock fixes the order of the samples. Sensor hardware latency was unmeasured.

Each model used the same evaluable windows. These required measured endpoints and available sensor histories both at issue and one second earlier. The delayed readings act as a comparison and can still carry information in a repeated squat. Of the eight people, six supplied the fitting data and two supplied the forecasts in each of four folds. Only fitting data set the scales or weights. No missing camera readings were manufactured for this comparison.

The linear fit used a squared-weight penalty of 1. The attention model used one encoder layer with 32 features, four attention heads and 64 features in the feed-forward layer. It had 9,314 trainable parameters. Each fit ran for 96 updates with 64 windows per update using AdamW at a learning rate of 0.002. The three attention predictions were averaged. The data file records exact error and coverage for every result.
