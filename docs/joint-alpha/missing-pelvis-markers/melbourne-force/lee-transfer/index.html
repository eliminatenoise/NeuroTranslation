Does treadmill force help on a new group of walkers?

A force plate measures how hard a foot pushes against the treadmill. We tested whether that reading helps predict how far markers on the pelvis will rise or fall 100 milliseconds later. We kept the models fitted in Melbourne fixed and tried them on a new group of walkers recorded by Lee and Lee.

Adding the latest total force lowered average error from 2.79 to 2.49 mm for all four pelvis markers and raised it from 2.62 to 2.74 mm for the rear pair. All four intervals for the main comparisons include zero. The test fell short of the rule for a consistent gain.

What carried over from Melbourne?

Melbourne put its two treadmill belts side by side. The Lee study put one belt in front of the other. We added the two calibrated vertical force readings to give the models one total load in either layout. The three fitted models used cameras alone, cameras plus the latest total force, or cameras plus total force from one second earlier. What was available before the forecast?

The Lee study filled gaps in its camera trajectories before export. A position stored at an earlier time can therefore carry information from later camera images. All 66,000 marker values in the tested segments were marked available, and all had a zero reconstruction residual. The file leaves open which of these positions the cameras saw before gap filling. The force readings were bounded on the stored clock. The latest force group ended before the last camera reading used for each forecast. The results describe a test on exported files. Forecasting from unfilled camera readings and measuring when data arrive in a live system are separate tests.

How the forecasts and scores were calculated

We set aside one recording to check the file and force calibration. All 11 planned test recordings contributed 131 matched forecasts per marker target. The walkers’ recorded task was treadmill walking at 4 km/h. We used a 15-second span starting 20 seconds into the recording. The first forecast waited for the history needed by the one-second control.

Each forecast used 80 camera readings through its last recorded frame at 100 frames per second. We added the two vertical loads at each force sample, then averaged groups of ten earlier samples. The target was the measured change in average marker height ten camera frames later.

We averaged absolute errors within each person, then gave every person equal weight. To estimate the intervals, we resampled people 5,000 times and kept each person’s paired comparisons together.

The summed-force model was developed on seven already-studied Melbourne walkers. In those reused development folds, mean error went from 2.54 to 2.07 mm for all four markers and from 2.72 to 2.28 mm for the rear pair. The Lee test used the frozen Melbourne weights.
