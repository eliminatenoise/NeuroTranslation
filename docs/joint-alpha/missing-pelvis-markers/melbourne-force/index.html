Can force from the treadmill sharpen a camera forecast?

A camera can see the hips rise and fall. A force plate under the treadmill measures the push against it. With the seven walkers from our earlier Melbourne comparison, adding the latest stored force lowered the ridge model’s average error from 2.54 to 2.24 mm for all four pelvis markers and from 2.72 to 2.46 mm for the rear pair.

Ridge fits a weighted sum of the past readings. Its latest-force version also beat the control that used force from one second earlier. In the attention model, which learns which readings to weigh, the intervals for adding force span zero. The attention model with the latest force had higher average errors than the earlier-stride replay.

What the model knew when it made a forecast

The camera stored 100 frames per second. Each forecast used 80 camera frames through its last reading and predicted the change in average height 100 milliseconds later. Two treadmill plates each stored 2,000 force readings per second. We averaged each plate’s vertical force in groups of 20 readings. The final group ended just before the last camera reading on the stored clock.

The shifted control uses the same force history from one second earlier. This tests whether the recorded timing helped the models. Establishing how a change in force causes a change in movement would require a separate experiment. Use in real time would also require measuring when force samples actually reach the model.

All eight methods used the same 917 forecast windows for each marker target. A shared camera clock selected the windows within the first 15 seconds of the recordings, beginning once enough history was available for the one-second control. Muscle electrical activity, or EMG, was outside this test.

What seven people can tell us

These walkers already appeared in the Melbourne walking comparison. Each time, we fitted the models and set their scales on six people, saved the forecasts for the seventh, and then checked that person’s later camera readings. We repeated this for each person in turn. An independent test of the gain would need new people.

All 917 tested camera histories contained all four exported marker positions. A separate reconstruction field, called a residual, was positive throughout 866 of the 917 histories. Every scored forecast met the residual rule at both endpoints for its chosen marker average. How C3D records residuals.

How the eight forecasts were compared

Replay finds a similar stretch in the recent camera history and borrows the movement that followed it. The speed estimate extends the last 50 milliseconds of change. These two reference methods use only the camera. We fitted ridge and attention three ways each, with cameras alone, with the latest force, and with the one-second-shifted force. The fitted models had the same architecture within a family. The camera-only versions received zeros in the two force channels.

We average absolute errors within each person and give every person equal weight in the average. The 90th percentile marks the start of the largest tenth of errors across all scored forecasts. Each difference compares methods on the same forecast windows. The 95% intervals come from resampling people 5,000 times while keeping each person’s two results together. These intervals apply to each comparison separately.
