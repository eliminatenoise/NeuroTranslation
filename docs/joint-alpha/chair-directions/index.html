Can a model trained to stand predict a sit?

Standing up and sitting down both move the hips between two heights. To test whether a model learned the movement or just one direction, we gave it earlier readings from people sitting down and asked how far four hip markers would move in the next tenth of a second.

With two camera positions, the stand-only model missed the sit-down displacement by 19.84 mm on average. Training on both directions brought that to 3.63 mm and lowered error for all 22 people. A model trained to sit averaged 3.10 mm. The comparison used 1,692 forecasts from 22 people.

On the earlier standing-up recordings, the joint model averaged 4.17 mm with two camera positions. The stand-only model averaged 3.63 mm. One model learned both directions. Training each model on one movement gave the lowest camera-only attention error for that movement.

How the comparison works

All forecast versions used the same earlier sensor windows and later camera endpoints within each comparison. Each test person counted equally after averaging their trials. Each model used 11,809 adjustable numbers. The reported forecast averages three training runs. The two-movement training gave the two directions equal weight.

The sit-down forecasts used new movement recordings from the same 22 people held out of the earlier chair-rise experiment. The rise scores reuse those earlier recordings and show how the two-movement training behaved on the original task. The authors filled gaps in the camera tracks and smoothed them before publication, a step that can use later samples. These comparisons concern the archived camera tracks.

Electrodes record muscle electrical activity, and floor plates record force against the ground. See the download for comparisons that add each stream. Those readings describe measured signals near the muscles and at the floor. They leave individual muscle force and live camera timing open.
