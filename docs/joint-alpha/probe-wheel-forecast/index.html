Do spikes help forecast the wheel?

A patterned patch appears on a screen. The mouse turns a small wheel with its front paws to bring the patch to the centre. A probe records brief electrical signals in the brain, called spikes. If we already know what the wheel has just done, how much do those spikes help us predict its next turn?

On the three test mice, adding spikes from the forecast trial made both models slightly less accurate on average. Both did better with the wheel and screen cue alone, or with spikes borrowed from another trial. The attention model did improve on the two validation mice.

How far off were the forecasts?

Six mice supplied 1,395 trials to fit the models. An earlier check used 458 trials from two separate mice, the validation group. The final test used 697 trials from three more mice. Each mouse had equal weight when fitting the models and when averaging errors in its group. In the test set, the recorded wheel angle changed in 671 trials and stayed the same between the two readings in 26.

Ridge learns what weight to give each input. Attention learns how much to draw from each earlier time block. We also tried guessing that the wheel would stay still or repeat its last 100 milliseconds of change. We scored the size of each miss in radians, a unit of angle.

What the forecasts could see

The forecast started 250 milliseconds after the recorded sound that cued the mouse to respond. Call that moment zero. Every fitted model saw the wheel’s recent motion and how old the last wheel readings were. It also saw the screen cue, which tells it which side the patch appeared on and how clearly it stood out.

−220 to −20 ms
Count earlier spikes in 20-millisecond blocks and record the share of qualified units that fired. Leave a 20-millisecond gap on the stored clock before the forecast starts.
−200 to 0 ms
Read the wheel’s history in 20-millisecond steps up to the forecast.
0 to +100 ms
Forecast how much the wheel angle changes. For scoring, take the last recorded angle at or before each endpoint and subtract the starting angle from the ending angle.

To test whether the spikes mattered at the right time, we also tried borrowing the spike counts from another trial in the same recording. The other trial had the same screen side and contrast, and its last wheel reading fell in the same age range. The forecast trial kept its own wheel history and cue.

Where the test applies

One planned validation session recorded conflicting wheel angles at the same timestamps, so it stayed out. The three test mice came from labs represented during training. The test used stored recordings and the 20-millisecond gap was a rule for selecting signals from those recordings. Live delivery time and whether spikes cause the wheel movement are separate questions.
