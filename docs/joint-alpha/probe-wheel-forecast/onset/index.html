Will the wheel start turning?

A mouse hears a beep and can turn a wheel with its paws to move a pattern on a screen. Fifty milliseconds after the beep, we predicted whether the wheel would turn by at least 0.006 radians, about a third of a degree, in the next 100 milliseconds. Could earlier bursts of brain activity, called spikes, help?

For the one test mouse, using spikes from the same trial raised the error of both models on quiet trials. Each model did better with the wheel and cue alone or with spikes borrowed from another trial.

A turn can count even if the wheel comes back

At the forecast, we took the last angle on record as our starting point. Then we looked for the first new reading to reach 0.006 radians from that point in either direction. A crossing counted even if the wheel returned to its starting angle before the 100 milliseconds were up. We also measured how far and in which direction the wheel had moved at the end.

Our main comparison used trials where the stored wheel angle stayed the same for the 200 milliseconds through the forecast. We checked every recorded reading, so a turn that went out and back within that window still broke the quiet history. This is quiet at the wheel sensor’s resolution. The mouse’s paws and body can still move.

How the International Brain Laboratory recorded wheel angles

Three crossings in 116 quiet test trials

We fit the models on 1,407 trials from six mice. Three other mice supplied 701 matched trials for a separate check, called validation. Their 120 quiet trials included 75 crossings. The test used 236 matched trials from one mouse in a lab outside training and validation. Across all its matched trials there were 25 crossings.

We squared the gap between the chance the model gave and what happened, using 1 for a crossing and 0 otherwise. The average is called a Brier score. Each mouse counted equally within its group. A smaller score is better.

Give the model the same wheel and a different spike history

Every model saw the recent wheel readings and the pattern’s side and visibility. A linear model combines inputs with fixed weights. The attention model can give different time blocks different weight. Each model gave a chance of crossing and a guess for the change in angle at the end.

We ran each model with wheel and screen alone, with spikes from the same trial, and with spikes borrowed from another trial of the same mouse and session. The borrowed trial had the same kind of screen pattern, the same quiet or moving history, and a similar time since the last wheel reading. All forecasts kept the wheel and screen inputs from the trial being predicted. The spike history covered 200 milliseconds and stopped 20 milliseconds before the forecast on the stored clocks.

A simple chance estimate used only how often crossings occurred in the training mice. Two simple wheel-angle guesses assumed either zero change or a repeat of the previous 100 milliseconds of change.

All 116 quiet test forecasts used an earlier angle because the wheel sensor logged no new reading in their 200-millisecond history. One crossing in the full test set returned to its starting angle by the end.

We saved the validation and test forecasts before scoring them with the later wheel readings. Only one of the three planned test labs could be scored. The other two test slots stayed empty.

  • Revised wheel positions lacked a source-verified timestamp partner.
  • Published probe clock maps disagreed, leaving the exact spike clock unresolved.

The forecasts describe one test mouse’s recorded wheel turns. They leave open whether spikes help predict movement at other test labs or in real time.
