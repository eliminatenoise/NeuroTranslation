The hand’s own history still beats the brain signal

NeuroTranslation tries to predict one recording of the body from another, most often of a movement: brain, nerve, muscle, force and motion, all from open datasets. This page gathers every one of its pages that runs in a browser. Most of them forecast: from a short stretch of a recording, they predict what a hand, a knee or a rat will do a fraction of a second later. Everything runs on your own computer, and nothing you give a page is sent anywhere. Pick one below: most open with an example, so you can start straight away.

What the studies found: a hand tends to keep moving the way it was moving, so what it has just done is the forecast to beat. In 25 small studies so far, each a numbered row of one table on the studies page, adding a brain recording taken with electrodes on the scalp (EEG) made that forecast worse in 8 of 13 comparisons. Start with an example

Several pages open with an example, so you can start with nothing of your own. Every “ahead” is a distance along a stored recording: nothing here is live.

  • One movement model · Fourteen kinds of forecast

    Forecast a wrist, a rat, both knees, the force under a foot, one marker on the skin, an arm after a cue, a sensor on the back, a muscle in the shin, the load on a pedal, a heel and the force under it, a walk after an instruction or balance on a beam

    Forecast how far a tracked wrist or rat will move in about a quarter of a second, the two angles of a wrist from its movement, forearm and scalp readings, both knees of a walker from four seconds of knee angles, how hard the forces around each knee push it to bend or straighten (its turning moment) from four seconds of leg angles, the force under a walker’s right foot, where one marker on a walking body will be 200 milliseconds later from nine earlier positions, or how three sensors on an arm will change after an instruction such as “bend my right elbow”; and, on pages of their own, how far a sensor strapped to a person’s back will turn in the next tenth of a second, how much a muscle in the shin, filmed by ultrasound, will lengthen or shorten, how the load on a pedal held still will change, where a walker’s heel will be and how hard that foot will press on the floor a tenth of a second ahead, and where a walker’s pelvis and heel will be a tenth of a second ahead after the instruction “as usual” or “fast but not running”, and how far a walker’s head and the base of the spine will move in the next eighth of a second after the view in a headset rotates. See how much four forearm electrodes change the angle forecast from the same wrist movement. Start from an invented example (the rat also has one recorded trip), or enter your own measurements. All fourteen tasks run on one shared model, each with its own input and output; the knee-moment version trained for its task has its own copy of the shared model. On the recordings, its distance forecast missed by 1.725 cm for the wrist and 1.310 cm for the rat; for the wrist’s angles and for the knees’ angles, models made for each did better; for the force under the foot, the single marker and the arm, simpler forecasts did; a straight-line fit did as well for the back sensor and better for the shin muscle, the heel and the force under it, and balance on a beam; and for the pedal’s load, it beat the simpler forecasts, and for the walking instructions it beat them too, though a copy with its shared part left untrained did better.

    Can brain activity sharpen a rat’s movement forecast?

    Step through one recorded trip and compare how far a rat moved with forecasts that use its earlier movement alone or also use firing from two brain regions.     Can four seconds of walking beat the last stride?

Three projects, one question

NeuroTranslation is one of three projects in one programme, Brain2Body, that ask the same question: what passes between the brain and the body? The brain sends orders out to the muscles, and the body reports back what it is doing and feeling. Each project comes at that traffic from a different side.

  • NeuroTranslation, whose pages are gathered above, predicts one recording of the body from another, most of them of a movement: brain, nerve, muscle, force or motion, recorded by scientists who share their data openly. Every prediction is checked against what was actually recorded, and so far a signal’s own recent past, above all a hand’s own recent movement, has been the forecast to beat. Every study and its result, on GitHub
  • NeuroSurge is a game for learning which part of the brain moves which part of the body. You look out from inside a body without skin. Turn your view to a coloured area of the brain in front of you, and the body part it controls moves; click a muscle and its area lights up. What the game’s brain does is made up by the game’s rules, not read from anyone’s brain. Play NeuroSurge in your browser
  • Interoceptomics is a proposed field of science that would measure what the body senses of its own interior, a heartbeat or a breath, and then ask which nerve connections carry it. None of its experiments has been run yet. Two of its pages run in your browser. The experiment guide walks through the proposed experiments one step at a time, each beside a 3D sketch you can turn. The atlas viewer is a 3D model of the left half of a human brain that you can turn, cut and measure. It is built from the Allen Institute’s published map of the brain’s regions: a map, not a measurement.

They are joined by a plan and a guide.

  • The game is meant to become the place where NeuroTranslation’s model is used. The plan: type or say an action, or move in front of your camera, and the body on screen does it, driven by NeuroTranslate 0.0, the one model NeuroTranslation is building to learn from many kinds of body signal at once. None of this is in the published game. A first draft is a proposed change to the game, not yet merged: commands you type or speak choose from a set of the game’s own movements, an arm or knee on screen follows yours as your camera sees it, and a map inside the game opens the recordings NeuroTranslation studies and its fitted models.
  • The experiment guide lays out the proposed experiments step by step, in two halves. Human movement (the “Small vision”) holds the movement tasks in people, where NeuroTranslation and the game meet; Animal learning (the “Big vision”) is Interoceptomics’ road map: six proposed stages of experiments in animals, in 22 numbered steps. Several of NeuroTranslation’s examples already sit inside the guide, among them two forecasts a quarter of a second ahead: the two angles of a person’s right wrist as they steer a cursor to a target, from three of its models, and where a rat on a W-shaped track will be.
