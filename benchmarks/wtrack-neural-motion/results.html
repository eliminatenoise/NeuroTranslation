Where will the rat be in a quarter of a second?

A camera followed rat JS14 along a W-shaped track. In nine later trips, a forecast using movement history missed the next measured position by 2.010 cm on average. Giving the same regression model access to 72 brain-cell firing histories increased its error to 2.237 cm.

What changed when we added spikes?

The aligned spikes gave a better estimate than firing borrowed from an earlier trip, yet the movement-only ridge was closer to the camera on seven of nine trips. The average increase in error from adding spikes was 0.227 cm. A descriptive interval from resampling blocks of four neighbouring trips ranged from 0.100 to 0.389 cm. Nine trips provide only about two nonoverlapping blocks that size.

All 123 forecasts used the same cutoff times. The target was the first available frame 250 to 290 milliseconds later. Distances were averaged within each trip, then across trips. Spikes alone had to predict absolute position without seeing earlier positions. The constant location came from training. Each plotted dot represents one scored camera sample. Connecting the samples would invent the route between them.

One rat, one run and two brain regions define this result. The archive supplies offline tracking estimates. Their original processing delay and the times when each cell could be reliably detected remain unknown. The rat’s position can be checked against the camera. Its intent and the causal role of a firing cell remain open.
