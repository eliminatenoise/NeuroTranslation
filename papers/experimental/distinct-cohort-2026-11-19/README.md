# What the forearm adds to a wrist forecast

What can a forearm electrode add once we can see where the wrist is going? The pointing experiment keeps the motion networks fixed and measures the gain from adding the signal to a matched correction. A separate KIN-MUS UJI experiment gives the model the task and clock and withholds the wrist's prior path.

- [NeuroTranslation · Technical paper](neurotranslation-technical.pdf) · [Forearm signals after the motion model has learned the turn, p. 23](neurotranslation-technical.pdf#page=23) · [A separate cohort and a different movement reference, p. 24](neurotranslation-technical.pdf#page=24)
- [NeuroTranslation · Explained edition](neurotranslation-explained.pdf) · [What the forearm adds after the model has learned the turn, p. 42](neurotranslation-explained.pdf#page=42) · [What a different set of wrists can tell us, p. 42](neurotranslation-explained.pdf#page=42)
- [Brain2Body · Research article](brain2body-article.pdf) · [Forearm signals after the motion model has learned the turn, p. 4](brain2body-article.pdf#page=4) · [A separate cohort and a different movement reference, p. 4](brain2body-article.pdf#page=4)
- [Brain2Body · Simple English edition](brain2body-simple-english.pdf) · [What the forearm adds after the model has learned the turn, p. 12](brain2body-simple-english.pdf#page=12) · [What a different set of wrists can tell us, p. 13](brain2body-simple-english.pdf#page=13)

Both comparisons score angles from stored recordings. They use different people, instruments and tasks. The KIN-MUS UJI angles come from calibrated glove readings filtered forwards and backwards. Each study has a separate reserved test. Feedback delivered during a live movement and learning that remains afterwards need their own tests.
