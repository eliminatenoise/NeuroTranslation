# Two papers, each written twice

**NeuroTranslation** asks what a recording of the brain or of a muscle adds to a forecast of a movement that already knows how the body has just been moving. It reports every study of the numbered table on [the studies page](../benchmarks/README.md), all 29 rows, a macaque study in which two networks lowered the average error and still failed the rule set for them, and the project's earlier attempts at reading movement from recorded brain cells. Then it describes the general model being built, NeuroTranslate 0.0, and the recording study that would test it.

**Brain2Body** starts somewhere else: let a machine guide a hand through a tune, then stop the guidance. What has the person kept? The paper proposes three ways to connect to a body (a pad on the brain, a strip along the spine, and a suit of fine gold electrodes that prick the skin), argues for trying them first in a small marsupial, and sets out the experiment and the results that would sink it. None of the three is built, and it reports no experiment on that question.

Each paper has a Technical edition, for readers who want the method, and a Simple English edition, which explains the same things in ordinary words and takes more pages to do it. Start with a Simple English edition.

## Not in either paper: what came after them

The NeuroTranslation paper ends its account of the general model with its first five fits, in section 7.4. Later that night the model was fitted on two datasets at once, and the paper says only that this happened and where it is listed. Everything below was published after the papers were written.

- **The general model's shared fit** learned a headset's tracking of people's wrists and a camera's tracking of a rat together, then a third task, the two angles of the wrist. Its forecast of those angles was later given a correction from four forearm signals, scored on six people the wrist studies had already used, each in turn by a version fitted on the other five: the error fell from 4.175° with a correction from the wrist's movement alone to 4.054° with the forearm signals, and an earlier model made for the wrist still did better (3.549°). Given a rat's brain-cell firing beside its camera track, its distance error fell from 2.805 to 2.778 cm, but the firing beat both its checks for only one of three rats. [The model and its comparisons](../models/README.md) · [Comparison figure (PDF)](../models/figures/wrist-correction.pdf) · [Methods, paired comparisons and source](../models/neurotranslate-0.0/forearm/) · [The firing comparison](../models/neurotranslate-0.0/population/)
- **Four studies published after the numbered table.** Two ask what a forecast gains from information available before the movement, against the body's own recent movement: [an instructor's finished words and the wrist one second later](../benchmarks/preceding-instructions/), and [earlier brain-cell firing and a rat's position a quarter of a second later](../benchmarks/wtrack-neural-motion/). In both, the extra information did not help. The other two search recordings of movement with words, one of the whole body and one of the hand. [The studies page](../benchmarks/README.md) lists all four.
- **A four-page supplement**, *How precisely do words identify a recorded movement?*, written to go with the NeuroTranslation paper but not part of it, reports those two searches. It explains why the timing of an action can be easier to find than the shape of the hand, and which question about guiding a hand, the subject of the Brain2Body paper, the studies leave open. [Read it online](https://eliminatenoise.github.io/NeuroTranslation/papers/language-and-observed-movement/language-and-movement.html) · [Download the four-page PDF](../docs/papers/language-and-observed-movement/language-and-movement.pdf) · [Editable source and evidence](../docs/papers/language-and-observed-movement/)
- **A stride from one word.** Enter **walk** or **run** and a small model generates a short stride from that word alone. On two held-out walks and one run, the average error of its body points was 89.82 mm, against 89.88 mm for the average recorded walk or run from training. [Generate a stride](https://eliminatenoise.github.io/NeuroTranslation/word-motion/) · [Recordings, method and results](../models/word-motion/)
- **A two-page supplement on where the body goes next.** One experiment continues a measured human stride for 600 milliseconds; the other forecasts how far a rat will move in a quarter of a second from its movement and the firing of its brain cells. The walk or run label helped place body points relative to the waist (71.9 to 69.1 mm), and correctly timed firing improved on both comparisons in only one of three rats. [Read the supplement](https://eliminatenoise.github.io/NeuroTranslation/papers/forecasting-movement/) · [Download the two-page PDF](../docs/papers/forecasting-movement/movement-forecast.pdf) · [Editable source and figures](../docs/papers/forecasting-movement/)
- **How much each finger will move.** The same person and recordings as the next item, but no model is given a recent glove reading: given the finger named on the screen, a straight-line model and a small transformer both did better than with the brain recording added (0.288 against 0.354, and 0.289 against 0.321). [The study](../models/finger-motion/README.md) · [Try the six models](https://eliminatenoise.github.io/NeuroTranslation/finger-motion/) · [The recorded brain-surface activity, by finger](https://eliminatenoise.github.io/Interoceptomics/recorded-cortex/)
- **A recording from the surface of the brain, and five fingers.** One person bent the finger named on a screen while a glove measured all five and electrodes on the surface of the brain recorded. Once the recent glove readings and the screen were known, adding the brain recording made both fitted forecasts of the glove worse, a straight-line model and a small transformer; keeping the last reading did best (0.1774, against 0.2849 for the straight-line model with the brain recording). [The study, with the original study's ethics statement](../models/cortical-glove/README.md) · [Try invented inputs](https://eliminatenoise.github.io/NeuroTranslation/finger-signals/)
- **A four-page supplement on treadmill walking.** The first experiment predicts where the knees were 15 stored frames later: replaying a stride from four seconds of knee history (3.54°) beat a transformer that saw 0.4 seconds of the joints (4.46°). Given the same four seconds, a transformer trained to correct the replayed stride did better than both (2.679°); the general model's knee task was a little closer than the replay (3.465°), a difference within the noise. In a second laboratory, with ten walkers measured by cameras, that transformer beat the replayed stride for only six. The third asks whether leg-muscle and knee-angle recordings agree on a walking rhythm when the clocks cannot establish the timing of individual steps: the same walk's rhythms differed by 0.027 cycles per minute, another walker's by 3.055. [Read the supplement](https://eliminatenoise.github.io/NeuroTranslation/papers/walking-rhythm/) · [Download the PDF](../docs/papers/walking-rhythm/gait-clocks.pdf) · [Editable source and results](../docs/papers/walking-rhythm/)

## Checked inside the project before the last fixes, and by nobody outside it

Each title page says "Working draft, 20 November 2026. Not peer reviewed.", and both are true.

Before the last round of fixes, the four PDFs had two checks inside the project, each by a checker who had not written the text. One re-read every number at the result file it came from, and checked the statements in words against their sources; the other read every page for plain words and for the voice the papers are meant to have. This edition carries the fixes; the fixed text has not been checked again. One kind of finding could not be settled without the cited papers themselves, and each paper says so above its references: figures quoted from other groups' papers were not re-read in the full papers. Nobody outside the project has reviewed the papers.

Where a paper and a result file disagree, the result file is right: [the studies page](../benchmarks/README.md) is written from the result files.

## Source, checksums and licence

- **Source.** [One ZIP](downloads/NeuroTranslation-Brain2Body-Papers-Source.zip) holds everything that builds the four PDFs: the TeX of both papers, the bibliography, the figure, the seven font files and the tools (91 files in all), the build script, and the results every printed number is taken from. Its README names what it cannot include, the TeX engine and the package files the engine needs, and gives the command. Built from it with the same engine and the same 415 package files (the ZIP lists each by checksum), the four PDFs come out byte for byte the same as the ones here.
- **Checksums.** [`current.json`](current.json) names the edition and gives the checksum of each ZIP ([papers](downloads/NeuroTranslation-Brain2Body-Papers.zip.sha256), [source](downloads/NeuroTranslation-Brain2Body-Papers-Source.zip.sha256)). [`current/manifest.json`](current/manifest.json) gives the checksum and page count of each PDF, and the page of each study.
- **Licence.** The project's own text is dedicated to the public domain under CC0 1.0. The datasets, simulators and fonts the papers rely on keep their own terms, and [`current/NOTICE.md`](current/NOTICE.md) lists each with its credit. All dataset credits of the repository: [docs/RIGHTS.md](../docs/RIGHTS.md).

Each new edition replaces these files at the same addresses. Earlier editions are in the repository's history.
