# NeuroTranslation

**What a hand has just done still beats a brain signal read at the scalp.**

That is where [25 small studies](benchmarks/README.md) stand, each a numbered row of the table further down this page. Each takes open recordings of people, measured and shared by others, and forecasts what a hand or an arm does a fraction of a second later: the angle of a wrist, the position of a palm, the force of a grip. A hand tends to keep moving the way it was moving, so its own recent past is a hard forecast to beat. A brain or muscle signal earns its place only by beating it.

- Adding a brain recording taken at the scalp (EEG) to a forecast that already had the hand's history made it worse in **8 of 13** comparisons. At the wrist, a quarter of a second ahead, the error grew from 4.05° to 4.52°. EEG met none of the 4 pass rules set for it before any scoring.
- Adding a muscle recording from the forearm (EMG) lowered the error in **10 of 18**. Only at the wrist was the gain clear of the spread between people. At the same wrist, the general model this project is building (more on it below) got worse when it was given EMG: its error rose from 5.43° to 5.87°, and stayed higher with any one of the three people left out (study 28).
- On the task it was fitted for, a forecast fitted to the hand's or arm's own recent past beat simply repeating the last value in **9 of 9** studies: 4.05° against 7.92° at the wrist.
- Change the task or the target, and a rule you could write in one line, such as "repeat the last value", beat the best fitted model in **6 of 6** settings.

NeuroTranslation is the larger attempt those studies belong to. Reach for a cup: your brain gives the order, the muscles of your forearm pull, and the hand moves. One act, written down three times, in nerve, in muscle and in motion. To translate is to take one of those records, predict another, and check the prediction against what the body did. It is one part of **Brain2Body**, the name of the whole programme and of its second paper, which asks what a person keeps once a machine stops guiding their hand.

What exists today is the studies, 22 [pages that run them or replay recordings in a browser](https://eliminatenoise.github.io/NeuroTranslation/START.html), two draft papers and a sister game, NeuroSurge.

What is still being built is the general model, **NeuroTranslate 0.0**: one model meant to learn from many kinds of body signal at once. On 20 November it had its first five fits to real recordings, all of them in the numbered table, and later that night a shared fit on two datasets at once.

- Two on people walking, with one kind of sensor strapped to the body that records how its speed changes (an accelerometer), from one open dataset. The errors are in the units the sensor stores.
  - One fit forecast five such sensors (torso, both arms, both legs) 120 ms ahead and was scored only on the four people it was fitted on. It met the rule set before fitting, to beat every other forecast for every person, at one sensor of five: the left leg, where its average miss was 1.837 against 1.929 for a straight-line fit made for that sensor.
  - An earlier version, fitted to the right arm alone, was scored on the two people study 26 had already scored. A straight-line fit beat it for both: 0.869 against 0.781.
- Three on people turning the wrist toward a target, from the recordings of studies 1 to 3, where electrodes on the scalp and the forearm and two angle sensors on the wrist recorded each movement together. They were scored on the three people those studies had already scored ([study 28](benchmarks/paired-wrist-forecast/)).
  - From the wrist's recent path alone, the forecast of the two wrist angles a quarter of a second ahead had an error of 5.432°.
  - With forearm EMG added, the error grew to 5.866°. With scalp EEG added as well it was 5.822°, and 5.826° when the same model was given another trial's EEG, so most of that small gain did not depend on the EEG coming from the right trial.
  - Study 1's small network, which reads only the wrist, still did best: 4.053°.
- The shared fit, published after the numbered table ([`models/`](models/)), learned two datasets at once: a headset's tracking of people's wrists as they worked with devices such as a printer or a coffee machine, and a camera's tracking of a rat running round a W-shaped track. One model, with a small final step for each dataset, forecast how far the tracked point would move in about a quarter of a second. Its average miss was 1.725 cm for the wrist and 1.310 cm for the rat. Fitted on one dataset alone, the same model did a little worse (1.782 and 1.342 cm), a difference within the noise, and a straight-line fit beat it on the rat (1.119 cm). Both test sets had been looked at before.
  - Later that night the shared fit learned a third task without changing its two distance forecasts: the two angles of the wrist in the recordings of studies 1 to 3 ([`models/neurotranslate-0.0/`](models/neurotranslate-0.0/)), from the wrist's recent movement, with forearm EMG and scalp EEG if they are given. It was scored on the six people those studies were fitted on, each in turn by a version fitted on the other five, so it is not a test. From the wrist's recent path alone its error was 4.503°; with forearm EMG, 4.291°; with scalp EEG as well, 4.491°. An earlier correction model made for the wrist (row 29 of the table below) did better on the same trials (3.549°), and the same model with its shared part left untrained, at random settings, did at least as well with every set of inputs.
  - Then a correction was fitted on top of that wrist-angle forecast and scored the same way on the same six people ([`models/neurotranslate-0.0/forearm/`](models/neurotranslate-0.0/forearm/)). Fitted on the wrist's movement alone, it brought the error down to 4.175°; given four forearm EMG signals as well, to 4.054°, lower for four of the six people. Forearm signals taken from another trial raised it to 4.419°. The earlier correction model made for the wrist still did better (3.549°, for five of the six), and, refitted on all six people, it is now a download of its own ([which wrist forecast to run](models/#which-wrist-forecast-should-i-run)). Both corrections also run on the shared model's page.

What does not exist yet: a test of the general model on people nobody has looked at before. So nothing here yet translates between nerve, muscle and movement. Nor is the model inside the browser app titled NeuroTranslate 0.0, the one that lines up your own recordings on one clock. Two of its fits run in a browser elsewhere, both listed below: the shared fit, with all three of its tasks, on a page of its own, and study 28's three wrist fits as a piece of code for programmers.

The shared model can also accept recorded brain-cell firing alongside a rat's recent camera track. Correctly timed firing lowered its average distance error from **2.805 to 2.778 cm**, but beat both movement alone and an earlier-trip firing control in only one of the three animals. This reanalysis used previously examined test recordings. [Run the fitted model and inspect its controls](models/neurotranslate-0.0/population/)

## A translation needs both texts from the same moment

NeuroTranslate 0.0 is meant to be a *large movement model*: one model that learns how nerve, muscle and movement go together, from four kinds of data.

| Kind of data | What it is | What it is not |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Neural recordings** | Electrical activity of the nervous system, at the places where the electrodes sat: brain, spinal cord or the nerves of the body. | A recording at one place is not a recording at another. EEG from the scalp says nothing direct about a nerve in the arm. |
| **Muscle recordings** | EMG: the electrical activity of muscle, picked up on the skin over it. | A recording of muscle, not of nerve. Not a force either: getting force out of EMG takes a second measurement or a mechanical model. |
| **Motion capture** | Where the body went: joint angles and hand positions, with their units and their clock. | The movement itself. It is a tracker's estimate, with the tracker's error. |
| **Physics-based simulation of the body** | Movement and force computed from a model of bones, joints and muscles. | Measured. A simulation is tested against a measurement of the same task, never the other way round. |

A fifth thing is needed, and it is not data. It is the pairing.

Suppose the motion recording belongs to a different reach. It can still teach a model what reaches look like. It cannot say where *this* hand was when *this* nerve fired. A translation can be checked only when both recordings come from the same person, the same movement and the same clock, and even two files that name the same clock can be offset from each other. Every study below uses signals that were recorded together.

The project's working presumption is that more data, and more varied data, makes a model that carries further. One fit has compared learning two datasets with learning one: the general model's shared fit learned a headset's tracking of people's wrists and a camera's tracking of a rat together. Against the same model fitted on one dataset alone, sharing lowered its error by 0.057 cm for the wrist and 0.032 cm for the rat, but the spread from resampling reached past zero in both (from 0.142 cm better to 0.003 cm worse for the wrist, from 0.081 better to 0.011 worse for the rat), and neither met the rule set in advance for a real gain ([its results](models/shared-motion/shared-motion-model/README.md)). It was scored on recordings kept out of its fitting, but the rat is the one it was fitted on, and the six wrist recording groups, already used in an earlier study, are not known to be people it had never seen. So the presumption is still untested on people the model has not seen.

## History did the work: the numbered table

Each row sets a reference forecast against a challenger that was given something extra. Some rows forecast a movement, some the force at a gauge, some the voltage of a force sensor. The studies published after the table are on [the studies page](benchmarks/README.md), in no row and no count here.

Every score is an error: how far the forecasts fell from what was measured. Lower is better. Rows 1 to 13, 28 and 29 are scored by the measure that punishes large misses, rows 14 to 23, 26 and 27 by the plain average miss, and rows 24 and 25 by a squared error in units of their own. Row 20 is the odd one: it compares the width of bands, not errors. Units change from block to block, so compare along a row, never down a column.

A *straight-line fit* is the plainest kind of model, a weighted sum of its inputs. A *learned forecast* is a small network that reads a stretch of recent history. A *transformer* is a kind of network that learns which of the earlier readings matter most to the forecast, the same kind that runs today's chatbots: study 26 tried a small one, and the general model is built round one too. The recordings a model is scored on were never used to fit it, except in rows 25 and 27. Row 29 keeps each person out of the fit that scores them, but its six people are the ones studies 1 to 3 and 28 were fitted on, so it is not a test either.

| 25 · [One network shared by two signals: first run](papers/current/neurotranslation-technical.pdf#nameddest=sec:self-forecast) | nobody held out | Repeat the last value: 0.0245 | One network shared by a muscle signal and a glove signal, after 990 training steps: 0.372 | **Repeating the last value**, on the network's own training recording. |

Two more counts. Adjusting a model to one person, one day or one group lowered the error in 5 of 6 comparisons, but in only 1 of them by more than 1% of the error. And 12 studies fixed a pass rule before any scoring: 8 failed it, 2 gave a split answer, 1 passed by a margin too small to tell from zero, and 1 passed on a question about band widths. Rows 27 and 29 are not among them: each set its rule before fitting, but scored it on people already used for fitting. No rule about adding a signal to a forecast was passed outright.

No test group is larger than 4 people, the 25 numbered studies draw on 9 datasets, and several rows score the same three or four people again. The brain signal in every study on people is EEG from 3 to 32 electrodes on the scalp: nothing here was recorded inside a human brain. And nothing was forecast live: every "ahead" is a distance along a stored recording.

[The studies page](benchmarks/README.md) has every number with its range and its controls.

## Run it yourself

In a browser: open the [start page](https://eliminatenoise.github.io/NeuroTranslation/START.html). A recording you open is read on your own computer and goes nowhere.

Offline: most readers have a ZIP that runs without a network, with a launcher for Windows, macOS and Linux, and the general model's five-sensor walking fit has a page that runs from your own computer. The folders of study 28 and row 29 have launchers that open their results pages, and in study 28's folder one Python command runs its three fitted wrist models on an invented example. [LAUNCHERS.md](LAUNCHERS.md) lists them all. The newer studies' pages carry launchers in their own folders: the hand search and the 3D recordings under [`docs/`](docs/README.md), the wrist forecast after an instruction, and the results pages of the rat study and the whole-body language study. So does the general model's shared fit: one launcher opens its page ([`docs/shared-motion/`](docs/README.md)), one opens the results of its wrist-angle task ([`models/neurotranslate-0.0/`](models/neurotranslate-0.0/)), and one runs the fitted model on two invented movements ([`models/shared-motion/`](models/shared-motion/), with Python 3 and NumPy). Row 29's refitted model has launchers in [`models/wrist-specialist/`](models/wrist-specialist/) that run it on an invented example, also with Python 3 and NumPy.

To score a study again from the original recordings: each folder under [`benchmarks/`](benchmarks/README.md) holds the study's plan and its results file, and several also hold the code that does the scoring. You fetch the recordings yourself, from their own archive. The palm study, for example, with Python 3.13:

```
cd benchmarks/scalp-eeg-palm-history
python -m pip install -r requirements.txt
python fetch_inputs.py --output ../iackd-data
python -m unittest -v
python run.py --input ../iackd-data --output ../availability-run
```

To build the app from source: `cd demo`, then `npm test` and `npm run build`. [demo/README.md](demo/README.md) says what the app does with a file you give it.

## Whose recordings these are

None of the recordings is ours. Eleven open datasets, measured by other people, carry the numbered table: nine behind its 25 studies, and two behind the two results reported only in the papers.

EEG-EMG-WristMotion · KIN-MUS UJI · the upper-limb dataset of Lucchetti and colleagues · IACKD · WAY-EEG-GAL · HYGRIP · Hyser · CEMHSEY GRASP · Daily and Sports Activities · LINK · Reach&Grasp

Four other datasets carry the 3D recordings and movement studies: HoloAssist (the hand search, the wrist forecast after an instruction and the shared fit), the KIT Motion–Language Dataset (the whole-body language study), Single Day W-Track Learning (the rat studies and the shared fit) and the CMU Graphics Lab Motion Capture Database (the 3D recordings, walk/run generator and stride forecast).

Katmah and colleagues’ treadmill dataset supplies the knee-angle forecast and the comparison of muscle and knee rhythms. Their recordings and the derived frequency estimates retain CC BY 4.0 attribution. [Dataset and authors](https://doi.org/10.13026/r0ea-7161)

Who made each one, its licence and the credit it is owed: [docs/RIGHTS.md](docs/RIGHTS.md). The numbered table's datasets are under CC BY 4.0, CC0 1.0, ODC-By 1.0 and MIT. HoloAssist is under the Community Data License Agreement (Permissive 2.0), Single Day W-Track Learning under CC BY 4.0, and the CMU recordings under Carnegie Mellon's own terms, which allow reuse but not resale; no open licence to pass on the KIT recordings has been established, so the whole-body language study publishes only numbers and summaries derived from them.

What is published here is mostly fitted models and summary results derived from the recordings. Two short excerpts of the numbered studies' recordings are included, each under its dataset's terms: one three-second trial of the wrist-pointing recordings, and 16 readings, with the reading recorded 120 ms after the last of them, from each of the five walking sensors in one clip, which the five-sensor page replays. The newer folders hold more, each under its own dataset's terms: the rat's positions and firing times on 34 trips round the track, five stretches of hand tracking with the sentences written about them, behind the hand search, the three 3D recordings, and measured walk and run excerpts in the stride viewers. The three-rat study also includes 16 selected trips from each of six rats. Its camera and firing data, fitted weights, and results retain the original dataset’s CC BY 4.0 attribution.

What the project itself wrote, text and code, is dedicated to the public domain under [CC0 1.0](LICENSE). That covers none of the recordings, nor the fonts inside the PDFs. The shared fit's weights are released under CC BY 4.0, with credit to both of its datasets, and to the wrist dataset of studies 1 to 3 for its wrist-angle task.

## The other two projects

**[NeuroSurge](https://github.com/eliminatenoise/NeuroSurge)** is a game for learning which part of the brain moves which part of the body. Its numbers are made up by the rules of the game. They are not forecasts from the studies here.

**[Interoceptomics](https://github.com/eliminatenoise/Interoceptomics)** is a proposed field of science: how the body senses its own interior, and how the nerve connections involved are arranged. Today it is a 3D atlas of the human brain that you can turn, cut and measure, and an interactive guide to the experiments it proposes, none of which has been run yet.
